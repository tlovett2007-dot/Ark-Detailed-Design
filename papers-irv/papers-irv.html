More Problems with the Finkel Ark

Esērum — the Verb Irving Missed

T. Lovett | Draft — in development | 2026

Irving Finkel's translation of the Ark Tablet (2014) concludes that the Babylonian flood vessel was a circular coracle. The central linguistic pivot is the Akkadian word kippatum, which Finkel reads as specifying a circular hull form. This paper argues that kippatum in its Old Babylonian mathematical context functions as a geometrical drafting term — describing a construction layout method, not the shape of the finished vessel. The circle is the template. Noah didn't build one.

The Ark Tablet — Disputed Footprint

The Ark Tablet is an Old Babylonian cuneiform tablet (c.1900–1700 BC) containing instructions for building a flood vessel. It was first examined by Irving Finkel of the British Museum in 1985 and fully translated by him in 2009, with publication in The Ark Before Noah: Decoding the Story of the Flood (2014). The tablet contains 60 lines of cuneiform script describing materials, dimensions, and construction.

The footprint of the vessel is disputed. Two interpretations exist:

The circular hull interpretation has been widely adopted by subsequent literature. It has not, to this author's knowledge, been subjected to a comprehensive independent philological critique. The only independent re-edition of the tablet is Wasserman (2020).

2. Finkel's Argument

Finkel translates the key instruction as: "Draw out the boat that you will make on a circular plan." He takes this as specifying that the hull form is circular — i.e., the vessel is a coracle, a round bowl-shaped craft familiar from ancient Mesopotamian river use.

The argument rests on three elements:

  1. The Akkadian word kippatum — translated as "circular plan" or "circle"
  2. The dimensional specifications — a vessel with area of 1 field (3,600 m²), sides 1 nindan high
  3. The construction materials — palm fibre rope on a wooden frame, consistent with coracle construction

Finkel built a one-third scale replica in 2014 to demonstrate the concept. The replica was large enough to accommodate "a few pairs of well-behaved animals." It was not seaworthy in any meaningful sense.

The circular hull interpretation has been widely adopted by subsequent literature — Becking and Dijkstra (2015), popular science writing, and museum exhibits. However, as the ChatGPT literature survey confirms: "there does not appear to be a published independent re-edition that supersedes or systematically critiques Finkel's reading." The coracle interpretation may have become accepted by repetition rather than by published debate.

3. Kippatum — Noun, Verb, or Method?

⚠ Open Item 2 — Full grammatical analysis of kippatum in the Ark Tablet line. Is it functioning as a noun ("a circle"), a verbal noun/infinitive ("to make circular"), or an adverbial phrase ("in a circular manner/layout")? The grammatical form determines whether the circle describes the hull or the construction method.

The key philological question is whether kippatum in this context specifies:

  • A hull form — "build the boat in the shape of a circle" → circular coracle
  • A construction method — "lay out the boat using a circular plan/template" → the circle is a drafting tool, not the final shape

In Old Babylonian mathematical texts, kippatum appears repeatedly as a geometrical term for the circumference or periphery of a figure — used in the context of laying out areas, calculating dimensions, and constructing geometrical figures. The word has a strong mathematical/drafting pedigree that predates its appearance in the Ark Tablet. Whether this pedigree supports Finkel's hull-form reading or an alternative layout-method reading is the central question of this paper.

The argument — as yet unpublished and awaiting development — is that kippatum in the Ark Tablet instruction functions as a drafting term: the builder is instructed to lay out the vessel plan using a circular reference — the standard Old Babylonian method for establishing a regular perimeter — not to build a circular vessel. The circle is the compass arc. The vessel need not be circular.

4. The Mathematical Pedigree of Kippatum

The word kippatum appears in several Old Babylonian mathematical tablets where its function is geometrical and specifically related to circular construction methods:

  • YBC 7243 — kippatum used in area calculations involving circular figures
  • YBC 11120 — kippatum in context of geometrical layout
  • BM 15285 — kippatum as a term in a series of geometrical figures — circumference/periphery sense

⚠ Open Item 3 — Full citation and text references for YBC 7243, YBC 11120, BM 15285. Confirm kippatum usage in each. Cite Robson (1999) and Neugebauer-Sachs (1945) as primary secondary sources for Old Babylonian mathematical terminology.

The mathematical pedigree of kippatum as a geometrical construction term is well established independently of the Ark Tablet. This is not special pleading — the word brings its mathematical toolkit to the Ark Tablet context. The question is whether that toolkit supports Finkel's interpretation or undermines it.

5. Statham (2006) — Prior Art

The first published proposal of a square Ark Tablet vessel was not made by an Assyriologist — it was made by engineer Dominic Statham in Creation magazine, shortly after Finkel's book. Statham's argument is geometric and engineering-based:

  • The tablet states the plan area is one iku (field)
  • It states length and breadth are equal
  • The 30 ribs are each 10 nindan (= 120 cubits) long
  • The bitumen quantity fits a 60×60×6m vessel far better than a 67.7m diameter circle
  • Therefore the "circular plan" refers to the layout process, not the finished hull

⚠ Open Item 4 — Full Statham reference: author, title, Creation volume, date, page numbers. Confirm the argument summary above is accurate.

Statham's conclusion is correct. His argument is engineering-based — the dimensions and materials don't fit a circular coracle. This paper's contribution is a philological argument that Statham does not make: the grammar of the kippatum line describes a construction layout method, not a hull form. Two independent lines of rebuttal, one engineering and one philological, converging on the same conclusion.

The Tablet Magazine — Partial Convergence

A later article in Tablet Magazine independently develops a similar idea — that the "circular plan" describes marking out a circle inside a 120×120 cubit square, with the square being the survey layout rather than the vessel. That author still ends up with a circular vessel, but explicitly distinguishes construction geometry from hull geometry. This is partial convergence: the author recognises the layout/hull distinction but does not pursue the grammatical argument to its conclusion.

Priority — No Assyriologist Has Proposed a Square Vessel

As of the ChatGPT literature survey (2026), no published Assyriologist has proposed a square vessel from the Ark Tablet. The closest published precursor is Statham (engineering argument). Wasserman (2020) examined the tablet independently but appears not to abandon Finkel's circular hull interpretation.

This paper's philological argument — that the grammar of the kippatum line specifies a construction method, not a hull form — is therefore a novel contribution to the Assyriological literature. If the grammatical case can be made convincingly from the Akkadian, it would be a stronger argument than Statham's engineering consistency argument, because it addresses the text directly rather than working backwards from the dimensions.

6. Cox — The Geometric Impossibility

Cox's paper "The Geometric Impossibility of Finkel's Round Ark" argues that a circular coracle at the dimensions specified in the Ark Tablet is geometrically and structurally impossible. Cox's central conclusion is correct — the vessel is not circular. However:

  • Statham (2006) established the conclusion before Cox — prior art not cited by Cox. Cox's central finding is Statham's finding, eight years earlier.
  • The kippatum philological argument — the strongest linguistic objection — is not developed in Cox
  • The vertical rib stacking impossibility — independently identified during peer review of Cox's paper; Cox's central structural argument is correct

This paper is complementary to Cox, not duplicative. Cox makes the geometric/structural argument (following Statham). This paper makes the philological/grammatical argument that neither Statham nor Cox makes. Together they constitute a comprehensive rebuttal of Finkel's circular hull interpretation from three independent directions: engineering consistency (Statham), structural geometry (Cox), and Akkadian grammar (this paper).

7. Wasserman (2020) — Independent Check

Nathan Wasserman, The Flood: The Akkadian Sources (Peeters, 2020), pp. 61–77 (Text 2.3.1) provides the first independent edition of the Ark Tablet. Wasserman collated the British Museum tablets himself in November 2018, copied the Ark Tablet for the first time independently, and provides a new edition, translation, and philological commentary. He is not merely repeating Finkel.

However, Wasserman explicitly thanks "Irving Finkel at the British Museum for useful discussions centring on the Ark Tablet" — his independence from Finkel's interpretation is therefore limited. He consulted Finkel during his work.

Wasserman's verdict: Roundy

Wasserman retains the circular interpretation. His translation of the key lines (Ark 6–8):

"The boat which you will build, I will draw it out (for you) — a circular plan: Her length and breadth should be equal, her base should be one ikû; her hull (lit. walls) should be one nindanu (high)."

He translates kippatum as "circular plan" — confirming Finkel's reading of that word. He does not discuss the layout-method vs hull-form distinction. He does not address the grammatical status of kippatum as a drafting term. He adopts Finkel's interpretation without independent philological argument on this specific point.

The Key Verb — esērum

What Wasserman's text reveals — without drawing the conclusion — is that the critical verb in the instruction is not kippatum at all. It is esērum — "I will draw it out (for you)."

Esērum means to draw, trace, or delineate. Enki's instruction is: "I will DRAW OUT a circular plan for you." This is a drafting instruction — Enki is describing the act of drawing a reference plan on the ground, not specifying the finished hull form. The circle is what Enki draws as a layout reference. The vessel that results need not be circular.

This is precisely the argument the Tablet Magazine article approaches without completing: the preliminary design is drawn as a circle on the ground; the circle is the construction reference, not the hull form. Wasserman's own translation — "I will draw it out (for you) — a circular plan" — contains the verb that undermines Finkel's conclusion. Irving missed the verb in his own text.

Tigay (1982) — Prior Art on kippatum

Wasserman's commentary on the parallel line in the Sippar recension (C, i 28') notes that Tigay (1982, 293) was the first to read [ki-ma k]i!-ip!-pa-ti "like a circle" in this context — based on the later version W 2'. The recently published Ark Tablet confirmed Tigay's reading. Tigay (1982) is therefore the first to identify kippatum in the flood boat context — eight years before Finkel first examined the Ark Tablet and thirty-two years before his 2014 publication.

What Wasserman Does Not Address

Wasserman's edition — despite being the most thorough independent treatment of the Ark Tablet — does not address:

  1. The grammatical function of esērum as a drafting verb vs a hull-specification verb
  2. The layout-method vs hull-form distinction
  3. The Old Babylonian mathematical pedigree of kippatum as a construction geometry term
  4. Statham (2006) or any alternative to the circular interpretation

The coracle interpretation has therefore been accepted by two scholars — Finkel (2014) and Wasserman (2020) — without either addressing the grammatical argument that esērum describes a drafting method, not a hull specification. Wasserman's independence from Finkel is structural (he examined the tablet himself) but not interpretive (he does not challenge Finkel's reading). The circular interpretation rests on one reading of kippatum and has not been subjected to a published philological challenge of the verb esērum.

8. The Structural Impossibility

Independent of the philological argument, the circular coracle at Ark Tablet dimensions is structurally impossible in ancient materials. The key problems:

  • Vertical rib stacking — the construction method described cannot produce a circular cross-section at 3,600 m² area
  • Palm fibre rope — insufficient tensile strength at the specified scale; the hoop stress in a 3,600 m² coracle would exceed the material capacity of palm rope by orders of magnitude
  • Seaworthiness — a coracle at this scale has no directional stability, no seakeeping ability in open water, and no structural integrity in wave conditions

These structural arguments are developed in Cox (2024). This paper notes them and refers the reader to Cox for full development, while concentrating on the philological argument Cox does not make.

9. Conclusion — Stub

Wasserman (2020) has answered one critical dependency: the circular interpretation is confirmed by the field's most thorough independent edition — but by adoption from Finkel rather than by independent argument. Wasserman does not address the grammatical case against the circular reading.

The paper's central argument — that esērum in Ark 6 describes a drafting method rather than a hull specification — remains unaddressed in the published literature. Finkel translated "I will draw it out (for you) — a circular plan." Wasserman retained this translation. Neither asked what esērum (to draw, trace, delineate) implies about the nature of the instruction: Enki is describing the act of drawing a reference circle on the ground as a construction layout method. The circle is the compass arc. The vessel need not be circular.

Three independent lines of rebuttal now converge:

  1. Engineering consistency (Statham 2006) — the bitumen quantities and rib dimensions fit a square vessel, not a circular coracle
  2. Structural geometry (Cox 2024) — the vertical rib stacking cannot produce a circular cross-section at the specified scale
  3. Akkadian grammar (this paper) — esērum is a drafting verb; kippatum describes the construction layout method, not the hull form; the Old Babylonian mathematical pedigree of kippatum supports the layout-method reading
