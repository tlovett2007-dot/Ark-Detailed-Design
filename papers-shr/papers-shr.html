1. Sohar — One Occurrence, No Consensus

Sohar (צֹהַר) occurs exactly once in the entire Old Testament — Genesis 6:16. No other occurrence exists from which to derive its meaning by context. Every translation is an interpretation. Every etymology is a proposal.

The word has resisted identification for 2,500 years. The LXX translators (c.250 BC) working at the Library of Alexandria — the greatest repository of ancient knowledge ever assembled — could not identify it. They retreated to a functional guess: episynagogon — a gathering or collection point. The word was already opaque when the greatest scholars of the ancient world attempted it.

This paper argues that sohar is antediluvian vocabulary — a pre-flood technical term for a specific architectural feature of a pre-flood vessel type. The feature did not survive the flood; neither did the word's referent. The honest conclusion, following Cohen (1972) on tebah, is: unknown — permanently. But engineering constraints from the Ark's mission allow us to characterise what the sohar must have done, even if we cannot identify precisely what it was.

3. LXX Episynagōn — A Functional Guess

The LXX text of Genesis 6:16 reads:

ἐπισυνάγων ποιήσεις τὴν κιβωτὸν καὶ εἰς πῆχυν συντελέσεις αὐτὴν ἄνωθεν τὴν δὲ θύραν τῆς κιβωτοῦ ποιήσεις ἐκ πλαγίων κατάγαια διώροφα καὶ τριώροφα ποιήσεις αὐτήν

Literal English: "Gathering-together you shall make the chest, and to a cubit you shall complete it from above; and the door of the chest you shall make from the side; lower, two-storied and three-storied you shall make it."

Episynagōn (ἐπισυνάγων) — present active participle of episynagō: to gather together, to collect, to assemble. The translators rendered sohar not as a noun naming a specific feature but as a participle describing an action or quality — a gathering-together. They understood the feature had something to do with collecting or assembling — light? air? — but could not identify it precisely.

This is the LXX at its most honest: not inventing an identification but describing a function inferred from context. Episynagōn is not a translation. It is a paraphrase of what the translators guessed the feature did. The contrast with their treatment of tebah (kibotos — confident box geometry imposed) and kopher (asphaltos — wrong material substituted confidently) is instructive. Where the LXX is confident, it is usually wrong. Where it hedges, as here, it is at least honest.

Note also that the LXX renders the three levels as κατάγαια διώροφα καὶ τριώροφα — "lower, two-storied and three-storied" — using architectural story terminology where the Hebrew simply has lower, second, third with no noun. The LXX supplies "storied" from context, as it supplies "gathering" for sohar.

The Root Meaning of Sohar

The Hebrew root צהר (tshr) means brightness, specifically noon-brightness or midday light. The cognate noun tsohorayim means "noon" or "midday" — the time of brightest light. Sohar from this root would carry the sense of a noon-brightness or midday-light feature — not merely a hole for light, but specifically the quality of bright overhead light characteristic of midday.

A vanilla rendering of Genesis 6:16a is therefore:

"Make a noon-brightness for it and finish it to a cubit from above..."

This is the most literal reading: make bright-noon-light for the vessel, and the finishing detail — a cubit from above — describes how this is achieved structurally. The feature is defined by what it does (admit noon-quality light) not by what it is (a window, a roof, a hole). Consistent with the functional vocabulary of the whole passage: tebah (life-vessel), kopher (covering), qinim (nests). The text specifies what things do, not what they are.

4. The Full Verse — "To a Cubit You Shall Finish It from Above"

צֹהַר תַּעֲשֶׂה לַתֵּבָה וְאֶל־אַמָּה תְּכַלֶּנָּה מִלְמַעְלָה
"A sohar you shall make for the ark, and to a cubit you shall finish it from above." — Genesis 6:16

5. Genesis 8:13 — "Noah Removed the Covering"

וַיָּסַר נֹחַ אֶת־מִכְסֵה הַתֵּבָה
"And Noah removed the covering (mikseh) of the ark." — Genesis 8:13

Mikseh (מִכְסֵה) — covering, lid, that which covers. This is a different word from sohar. The natural reading is that Noah opened the sohar hatch covers — the closeable panels over the sohar opening — to look out and assess conditions.

If the sohar were the entire weatherdeck — 1.6m of integrated stressed-skin timber construction, mortise-and-tenon jointed, trunnel fastened, steel bolted — "removing the covering" would require weeks of demolition work by eight people. The text implies a single action, immediately done.

If the sohar is a continuous clerestory opening with closeable hatch panels (mikseh), "removing the covering" means opening the hatch panels along the clerestory strip. A single person working around the hull could accomplish this in under an hour. The text is consistent; the weatherdeck interpretation is not.

6. Engineering Constraints

Whatever sohar means, the engineering requirements of the Ark's 371-day mission provide hard constraints:

RequirementImplication
Light — 371 days, no electricitySubstantial daylight admission required on all three decks
Ventilation — thousands of animals producing CO₂, methane, ammoniaSubstantial air exchange required — a single small window is wholly inadequate
Watertight in storm seasAny opening must be closeable or protected from wave action
Passive — 8 people cannot manage complex ventilation systemsNatural convection preferred; no mechanical systems
Weathervaning — waves from astern in Phase 3Openings on lee side (bow) protected; openings must survive storm conditions

A single small window satisfies none of these requirements adequately. The engineering constraints point strongly toward a substantial, continuous, protected opening.

7. The Clerestory Interpretation

A continuous clerestory opening running around the hull just below the roof line — one cubit (524mm) deep — satisfies all engineering constraints simultaneously:

  • Light — admits daylight to the upper deck directly; reflected light reaches lower decks
  • Ventilation — opening perimeter ~157m × 2 sides × 0.524m = ~165m² of potential opening area; even partially open provides substantial air exchange by natural convection
  • Weather protection — roof overhang of one cubit ("finished from above") protects the opening from direct rain and wave spray
  • Closeable — hatch panels (mikseh) can be closed in extreme conditions; "Noah removed the covering" = opened the hatch panels
  • Passive — natural convection through clerestory requires no crew management in normal conditions

"To a cubit you shall finish it from above" — the roof overhangs the opening by one cubit, providing weather protection. This is the most natural engineering reading of the clause.

8. Antediluvian Vocabulary

Sohar belongs to the cluster of Genesis 6 words whose etymology is permanently unresolvable because the referent is pre-flood. See The Words That Didn't Survive the Flood for the full framework.

The word describes a specific architectural feature of a pre-flood vessel type — a feature that may not have had a post-flood equivalent in ordinary boat construction. Noah's tebah was unique; its architectural vocabulary was unique with it.

The Babylonian accounts provide a parallel:

AccountOpening mentionedWord used
Genesissohar (6:16); chalon "window" (8:6)Pre-flood technical term + post-flood generic
Gilgamesh XIWindow (nappašu) — opened after flood (XI:135)Generic Akkadian word for window/opening
Eridu Genesis (Ziusudra)Window — opened after floodGeneric Sumerian term

Genesis uses sohar — a specific pre-flood technical term — for the construction specification, then switches to chalon (generic "window") when Noah opens it after the flood. The Babylonian accounts use only generic terms throughout. Genesis preserves the original pre-flood vocabulary; the Babylonian accounts substitute post-flood generic words. This is consistent with the antediluvian vocabulary framework.

9. Comparison with Babylonian Accounts

See table in Section 8 above. The key distinction: Genesis 6:16 uses sohar — specific, opaque, pre-flood — for the construction command. Genesis 8:6 uses chalon — generic, transparent, post-flood — when Noah actually opens the window. The construction specification preserves the original term; the narrative action uses the word any post-flood reader would understand.

This two-word pattern — specific pre-flood term in the specification, generic post-flood term in the narrative — is consistent with Moses preserving the original antediluvian vocabulary in the technical specification while using accessible language in the narrative. It is not consistent with a post-flood author inventing both.
