The Hebrew word tebah specifies three dimensions — 300 × 50 × 30 cubits — and nothing else. It carries no geometric information about cross-sectional hull form. (For the philological argument see papers-tbh.) Once etymology is set aside, hull form is determined by one discipline: naval architecture.

This paper applies naval architecture to the Genesis 6–8 specification. It evaluates two hull forms that have appeared in the creationist literature against the criteria the operating environment demands.

The Two Hull Forms

Hull Block coefficient Cross-section Proponents
Box Ark Cb ≈ 1.0 Rectangular — vertical sides, flat bottom, sharp bilge corners Whitcomb & Morris (1961), CMI, Hong et al. (1994)
Lovett / AiG hull Cb = 0.87 Curved — asymmetric bow/stern, rounded bilge, stern trim Lovett (2004), Ark Encounter (AiG, 2016)

The Lovett/AiG hull is not a conventional ship form. Cb = 0.87 is extremely full by any shipbuilding standard — closer to a barge than a clipper. The departure from box geometry is the minimum required to achieve passive weathervaning. Every concession from Cb = 1.0 is driven by a specific engineering requirement, not by an aesthetic preference for ship-shaped vessels.

Other proposed hull forms — Finkel's coracle, the Gilgamesh cube — are not evaluated here. Both are addressed in papers-tbh §4 and papers-irv. Both fail the weathervaning criterion at the degenerate extreme.

The Operating Environment

Hull form evaluation requires a defined operating environment. The Genesis 7–8 account specifies the following constraints:

  • Duration: approximately five months afloat on open water (Genesis 7:24, 8:3)
  • Crew: eight people, none experienced mariners; no propulsion, no steering
  • Destination: none — the vessel drifts under divine providence (Genesis 8:1)
  • Draft constraint: 15 cubits (7.86m) — Genesis 7:20
  • Critical event: Genesis 8:1 — a specific directed wind introduced at the flood's peak

A vessel with no propulsion, no steering, and no experienced crew has one survival requirement above all others: it must manage its own heading passively. An unpowered vessel caught beam-on in a trough has no recovery option.

Voyage Sea State Progression

The sea state was not uniform across the voyage. Three phases are distinguishable:

Phase Sea state Primary wave type Weathervaning relevance
1 — Flood rising Tsunamis, rising water Long-wavelength, low amplitude in deep water — essentially harmless to a floating vessel Low — tsunami energy is distributed through the full water column; surface amplitude trivial
2 — Open ocean, Mabbul Confused sea Wind waves from multiple directions; no dominant heading Low — no single wind direction to align to; Hong et al. confused sea assumption applies here
3 — Genesis 8:1 wind Directed wind sea superimposed on mature swell Steep locally-generated wind waves (directional) + longer background swell (less steep, less dangerous) Critical — directed wind both generates the dangerous waves and provides the weathervaning solution

The Hebrew word mabbul carries an intensifying connotation — the flood worsens toward its peak. This is consistent with Phase 3 representing the most severe sea state of the voyage, arriving at Genesis 8:1. The hull form that survives Phase 3 is the design requirement.

The Primary Design Driver — Passive Weathervaning

An unpowered vessel in a directed wind sea will adopt one of two headings:

  1. Bow or stern to wind — if hull form generates a passive yaw moment. The vessel rides before the sea or heads into it. Survivable.
  2. Beam-on — if hull form is symmetric end-to-end. The vessel lies in the trough. At the mercy of breaking crests. Not survivable over five months.

Passive weathervaning requires asymmetry between bow and stern — sufficient difference in above-water profile, underwater resistance, or both, to generate a net yaw moment when wind and waves act on the hull. This is a function of length-to-beam ratio and end form geometry.

Every ocean-going sailing vessel that survived open ocean storms did so by maintaining forward sail area — storm canvas set well forward, or bare poles with wind load concentrated on bow structure. In survival conditions the mechanism is identical to passive weathervaning: asymmetric wind load forward of amidships drives the bow downwind and holds the vessel in a following or near-following sea.

The Ark Encounter bow form achieves this permanently and without crew input. The forward structure presents a larger above-water profile than the stern — a fixed, rigid equivalent of storm canvas. No decision required, no canvas to deploy, no crew needed at the critical moment.

For the full weathervaning argument, sail area analysis, and historical vessel comparisons see papers-vane.

The Box Ark Has No Preferred Heading

A rectangular hull with vertical sides and square ends presents identical resistance fore and aft. In a directed wind sea it has no mechanism to generate a passive yaw moment. Beam-on is not the worst-case scenario for a box ark — it is the default. Any other heading is temporary and unstable.

This is the single most important failure of the box hull form. Roll stability, structural capacity, and seakeeping can all be engineered around. A hull that defaults to beam-on in open ocean conditions cannot be made survivable by any passive means.

The Hong et al. (1994) Proportions Study

Hong et al. evaluated 13 hull forms against the Genesis 300 × 50 × 30 cubit proportions across multiple naval architecture criteria. Their result: the Genesis proportions ranked 3rd overall.

Two important observations about this result:

1. The confused sea assumption

Hong et al. used a confused sea — waves arriving from multiple directions simultaneously with no dominant heading. Under confused sea conditions weathervaning is not possible (there is no single wind direction to align to), so it was not evaluated as a criterion. The two hull forms that outranked the Genesis proportions in Hong's analysis were shorter and wider — lower L/B ratio.

Short, wide hulls perform well in confused sea roll analysis. They are a poor design choice for directed wind sea survival: lower L/B ratio reduces the passive yaw moment available from bow/stern asymmetry. The fat, short hulls that beat Genesis proportions in Hong's test tend toward the Gilgamesh degenerate case — symmetric, no preferred heading, beam-on default.

Hong's confused sea assumption is appropriate for Phase 2 of the voyage. It does not capture the Phase 3 Genesis 8:1 condition, which is the design-governing sea state.

2. What 3rd place actually means

The Genesis proportions ranked 3rd in confused sea performance while retaining the L/B ratio necessary for passive weathervaning in directed wind sea. The two hulls that outranked them gain confused sea performance at the cost of weathervaning capability.

A hull that ranks 3rd in confused sea and 1st in directed wind sea is not a compromise. It is a design that maintains adequate performance across the full voyage envelope while excelling at the critical condition. The shorter/wider hulls are locally optimal for one phase. The Genesis proportions are globally adequate for all phases.

For full critique of Hong et al. methodology see papers-hong.

The Volume Objection

Whitcomb & Morris (1961) and subsequent creationist literature chose the box hull form primarily to maximise animal-carrying volume. The argument: maximum Cb = maximum interior space = maximum animal capacity. This argument has three independent problems.

1. Wrong cubit

The box ark volume argument has consistently been calculated using the 18-inch cubit (457mm) — the shortest plausible cubit in the literature. This research uses the Royal Egyptian Cubit of 524mm, confirmed as the most defensible choice (see hull-cubit).

Volume scales as the cube of the linear dimension. The ratio:

(524 / 457)³ = 1.52

The Royal Egyptian Cubit ark has approximately 52% more volume than the 18-inch cubit ark — before hull form enters the calculation.

2. Wrong hull thickness

Hong et al. assumed a hull skin thickness of 300mm — approximately one plank at modest scantling. The actual structural requirement for a 157.2m timber vessel is substantially greater. The Lovett hull specification (see hull-skin):

Location Build-up Total thickness
Bottom 2 sacrificial + 4 structural planks × 127mm + 812mm frames + 2 × 114mm ceiling ~1.8m
Sides 1 sacrificial + 4 structural planks × 127mm + 812mm frames + 2 × 114mm ceiling ~1.7m

Hong's 300mm assumption dramatically overstates the usable interior volume of a structurally adequate box hull. A box ark with correct scantlings loses far more interior volume to hull structure than Hong's figures indicate.

3. Wrong constraint

Volume is not the binding constraint on animal capacity. See papers-fit for the volume-first capacity analysis. The Genesis proportions — even at Cb = 0.87 and correct scantlings and REC cubit — provide adequate volume for the required animal complement. The capacity argument for the box hull form was solving a problem that does not exist.

The combined effect: the cubit correction alone more than compensates for the Cb difference between box (1.0) and Lovett hull (0.87). The volume objection to the ship-form was always built on the shortest plausible cubit and the thinnest plausible hull. Correct either assumption and the objection dissolves. Correct both and the ship-form has more usable volume than the box ark on the short cubit.

The box hull passes two criteria outright (GM, draft) and is marginal or failing on six others. It fails the primary design driver — passive weathervaning — without any possibility of passive recovery. The Lovett/AiG hull passes all criteria. The one criterion the box hull wins unambiguously — buildability simplicity — is outweighed by the fact that ancient timber shipbuilders universally rejected the box for ocean use when they had the choice.

The Archaeological Verdict

No large timber ocean-going vessel in the archaeological or iconographic record has a box hull. The evidence spans every ocean and every continent where timber shipbuilding occurred — Phoenician, Minoan, Greek, Roman, Viking, Chinese, Indian Ocean, Polynesian — across 4,000 years of documented history. Every independent tradition converged on curved hulls for open water.

This convergence is not coincidental. It reflects a fundamental constraint of timber construction at scale: the box is the carpenter's default (straight cuts, right-angle joints, flat panels), but open water demands the curved hull. Every ancient shipbuilder independently chose to fight the wood — because the sea demanded it.

The box reading of tebah requires Noah to have built something that no subsequent timber shipbuilder anywhere in the world independently replicated for ocean use. The accumulated engineering verdict of the ancient world is against the box. See papers-tbh §12 for the full archaeological survey.

Conclusion

Tebah is silent on hull form. Naval architecture is not.

The Lovett/AiG hull (Cb = 0.87) represents the minimum departure from box geometry required to achieve passive weathervaning — the single non-negotiable requirement for an unpowered vessel on five months of open ocean with no experienced crew. Every geometric concession from Cb = 1.0 is driven by that requirement. The result is a hull that is extremely full by any shipbuilding standard, concedes as little volume as engineering allows, and passes every naval architecture criterion the operating environment demands.

The box hull fails the primary criterion without recovery. The volume argument advanced in its favour was built on the shortest plausible cubit and the thinnest plausible hull. Correct either assumption and the argument dissolves.

The AiG Ark Encounter is the architectural consequence of taking the naval architecture seriously.
