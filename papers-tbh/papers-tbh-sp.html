Noah's Ark — Naval Architecture Research

Spoelstra (2013/2020)

The Most Recent Peer-Reviewed Treatment of Tebah — An Analysis

T. Lovett | 2026 | Part of papers-tbh

Joshua Joel Spoelstra's Life Preservation in Genesis and Exodus: An Exegetical Study of the Tebā of Noah and Moses (Contributions to Biblical Exegesis and Theology 98; Leuven: Peeters, 2020 — originally PhD dissertation, Stellenbosch University, 2013) is the most recent peer-reviewed academic treatment of tebah in the literature. It is a serious work from a credentialled institution with a serious publisher. Its treatment of tebah etymology therefore repays careful analysis.

1. Spoelstra's Thesis in Summary

Spoelstra examines tebah across its 28 occurrences in Genesis 6–9 and Exodus 2, and extends the analysis into Exodus 15. His central claims:

  • Tebah is an Egyptian loanword, derived from ḏbȝ.t — container, coffin, divine shrine (Götterschrein)
  • The word functions as a contra-coffin — a polemical inversion in which the protagonist enters what appears to be a death-vessel and emerges into new life while everyone outside dies
  • Noah's ark is a Götterschrein — a sacred structure atop a mountain (Ararat as ziggurat), where Noah offers sacrifice and receives covenant from God
  • Diachronically, tebah reflects Israel's exile experience — entering death, emerging to new life in a new world
  • Conclusion: "hbt is a terminus technicus for a life-preserving receptacle in the Hebrew Bible"

The theological argument — particularly the Exodus 15 extension and the temple ideology — is original and has merit on its own terms. The etymological argument is another matter.

2. The Chess Moves — Spoelstra on Cohen and Yahuda

The sequence of moves Spoelstra makes in his etymological argument is worth tracing carefully.

1
Acknowledges Cohen as the central authority. Spoelstra summarises Cohen (1972) accurately: "Cohen is not satisfied with any of the proposals, Akkadian or Egyptian, for a concrete etymological or philological adjudication to be made."
2
Quotes Cohen's demolition verbatim. From the dissertation (p.197):
"The Egyptian cognates usually given as evidence for the alleged Egyptian origin of hbt are ḏbᵌt and tbt which are translated 'Palast o.a; Schrein, Sarg,' and 'Kasten' respectively. However, never are either of these words used in Egyptian texts for boats."Cohen (1972): 39 — quoted in Spoelstra (2013): 197, with Cohen's own underlining retained
3
Cites Erman-Grapow at the correct pages. Footnote 42 cites Wörterbuch 5:261 and 5:561 — the very pages that, on direct examination, contain no Hebrew tebah connection. Spoelstra's citation confirms the Egyptological abandonment.
4
Introduces Yahuda as a rebuttal. Spoelstra presents Yahuda's rescue attempt — that dp.t (ship) etymologically underlies ḏbȝ.t (coffin/shrine), making ḏbȝ.t a legitimate nautical cognate — as "Yahuda's rebuttal to the classic critique as represented by Cohen."
5
Accepts Yahuda without argument. One sentence: ḏbȝ.t is "a legitimate cognate to convey a type of caïque." No philological justification. No engagement with Spiegelberg (1929), who demolished this exact move. No engagement with Cohen, who cited Spiegelberg and closed the door.
⚠ Fatal gap — the authority he just quoted (Cohen) had already foreclosed this move via Spiegelberg.
6
Concludes Egyptian etymology stands. Tebah is an Egyptian loanword from ḏbȝ.t.
7
Concludes functional meaning. "hbt is a terminus technicus for a life-preserving receptacle." — Cohen's own conclusion, independently restated 41 years later.
The Achilles heel. Spoelstra cites Cohen's demolition of Yahuda accurately. He then accepts Yahuda anyway — without rebutting Cohen, without engaging Spiegelberg, without explaining why the authority he just quoted should be disregarded on this specific point. The gap between moves 3 and 5 is the thesis's fatal weakness. An examiner who had read Cohen — which Spoelstra demonstrably had — would ask one question: "On what grounds do you accept Yahuda when you have just cited the authority that demolished him?" No answer is given.

3. The Selective Use of Cohen

Spoelstra uses Cohen in two incompatible ways across the thesis:

Where Cohen supports him — the functional life-preservation conclusion. Here Cohen is authoritative, decisive, cited approvingly. Spoelstra's own conclusion ("terminus technicus for a life-preserving receptacle") is essentially Cohen's conclusion restated in different words.

Where Cohen undermines him — the demolition of Yahuda via Spiegelberg. Here Cohen is quietly set aside. No rebuttal is offered. The thesis simply proceeds past the objection.

This is not a permissible scholarly move. If Cohen is authoritative enough to underpin the functional conclusion, he is authoritative enough to foreclose Yahuda. Spoelstra implicitly accepts Cohen's authority throughout — except at the one point where Cohen directly contradicts his etymological position.

The honest path was to rebut Cohen on Yahuda explicitly: show why Spiegelberg was wrong; show why Cohen was wrong to accept Spiegelberg; show why dp.t → ḏbȝ.t is a legitimate etymological move despite both. No such argument is made.

4. The Internal Contradiction

Spoelstra's two conclusions sit in unacknowledged contradiction:

  • Egyptian etymology: tebah derives from ḏbȝ.t — coffin, funerary chest, death-vessel
  • Functional conclusion: tebah is a life-preserving receptacle

He attempts to bridge this with the "contra-coffin" concept — the protagonist enters what looks like a coffin but emerges into new life. This is theologically creative. But it does not resolve the etymological problem. Cohen's objection was not semantic — it was that ḏbȝ.t is never used for boats in Egyptian texts. A polemical inversion of a coffin word is still a coffin word whose donor language doesn't use it for watercraft. The theological reading cannot rescue the linguistic foundation.

5. The Basket — One Observation That Sinks the Thesis

Spoelstra's contra-coffin / Götterschrein theology is constructed almost entirely around Noah's ark. The ark sits atop Ararat like a ziggurat. Noah offers sacrifice. God is soothed. A covenant is received. Sacred space, temple ideology, exile and return — the argument has internal coherence for Genesis 6–9.

Then there is Moses' basket.

Tebah occurs twice in the Hebrew Bible: 26 times for Noah's vessel, twice for Moses' basket (Exodus 2:3, 2:5). The word must work for both occurrences or it works for neither. This is the test Cohen established and Spoelstra accepts.

The basket has none of it. No ziggurat. No mountain. No covenant. No sacrifice. No divine shrine. No cosmic death-waters on a world-ending scale. No polemical counter to a grand funerary tradition. Just a desperate mother concealing a baby in a papyrus basket among the reeds of the Nile, watched over by his sister.

Spoelstra's elaborate theological superstructure applies to exactly one of the two occurrences of tebah. Cohen's simple functional definition — life-preserving vessel — applies to both without remainder.

The basket sinks the thesis.

6. What Spoelstra Inadvertently Proves

Despite its etymological problems, Spoelstra's thesis is valuable as a document of the literature's state. It inadvertently establishes three things:

Cohen is unavoidable. A 300-page PhD thesis devoted to tebah, written 41 years after Cohen, cannot get past him. He is cited, quoted, engaged (partially), and ultimately confirmed. No subsequent scholar has rebutted him. Spoelstra is the most credentialled attempt, and it fails.

The best available rebuttal fails. Yahuda's rescue — the only substantive challenge to Cohen's Egyptological demolition — was foreclosed by Spiegelberg in 1929, confirmed by Cohen in 1972, and remains foreclosed. Spoelstra's acceptance of Yahuda without engaging Spiegelberg is not a rebuttal; it is an assertion.

Cohen's conclusion is correct. Spoelstra's own functional conclusion — "terminus technicus for a life-preserving receptacle" — is Cohen's conclusion. He arrives there by a broken road, but he arrives there. The most recent peer-reviewed treatment of tebah confirms, inadvertently, what Cohen established in 1972.

7. Verdict

Spoelstra's thesis makes a genuine original contribution in its Exodus 15 extension and temple ideology argument. That work stands independently of the etymological question and deserves engagement on its own terms.

The etymological argument does not stand. It accepts a move (Yahuda) that had been demolished before it was published (Spiegelberg 1929, Yahuda 1933), confirmed as demolished by the thesis's own primary authority (Cohen 1972), without offering any rebuttal. It produces a conclusion (life-preserving receptacle) that is Cohen's conclusion — arrived at despite, not because of, the Egyptian etymology argument.

The basket observation requires one sentence and demolishes the contra-coffin framework entirely.

Cohen (1972) remains unrebutted. Tebah's etymology is undetermined. The shape of Noah's ark is not in the word.
