HALOT on Tebah

The Hebrew and Aramaic Lexicon of the Old Testament — Parsed in Full

Source: Hebrew and Aramaic Lexicon of the Old Testament (HALOT), Koehler, Baumgartner and Stamm, 5 vols. Brill, 1994–2000. Screenshot from Logos Bible Software, July 2026.

HALOT is the modern replacement for BDB — the gold standard current Hebrew lexicon, published nearly a century after BDB (1906). Compare its tebah entry with BDB's entry to see how the field has (and has not) progressed.

Key finding: HALOT independently reaches Cohen's conclusion — etymology undetermined, decision open — without citing Cohen (1972). Two independent lines of evidence converge on the same answer. HALOT also states explicitly that the ANE flood vessel was "a huge ship, or simply a ship" — not a box.

Section A — Preliminary Remarks

A.1 — The Two-Word Problem

"while Gesenius-Buhl Handw., Zorell Lex., and König Wb. recognise only one sbst. תֵּבָה, KBL differs in distinguishing between I תֵּבָה chest, and II תֵּבָה palace, ark."

sbst. = substantive (noun).
KBL = Koehler-Baumgartner Lexicon (the predecessor to HALOT). KBL had proposed splitting tebah into two different words — I: chest, and II: palace/ark — on the basis that ekallu in Gilgamesh means "palace." HALOT here is reviewing that proposal.
"This is probably dependent on the use of ekallu meaning palace in the Epic of Gilgamesh ix: line 95, which has been wrongly identified with the ark of Utnapištim."

HALOT rejects the two-word split. The ekallu = palace identification was an error — ekallu in Gilgamesh XI:95 refers to Utnapištim handing over his palace to the ferryman, not to the vessel itself. This is Cohen's point in footnote 28 — Borger's translation shows this. HALOT independently reaches the same conclusion.

Result: Tebah is one word, not two. The attempt to split it into chest/palace homonyms is abandoned.
"The attempt to distinguish two different words תֵּבָה can therefore be abandoned; on this see also W.H. Schmidt BK 2: 68f."

HALOT closes the two-word debate definitively. One word, two uses. The two-homonym theory (Koehler/KBL) is rejected.

A.2 — Egyptian Etymology: Open Question

"The sbst. is a loanword from Egyptian and may be derived either a) from ḏb't shrine, sarcophagus; or b) from tbt chest"

HALOT presents two competing Egyptian candidates — not one as BDB did:

a) ḏb't — shrine, sarcophagus. Note: sarcophagus, not merely chest. This is the more specific coffin/shrine meaning.
b) tbt — chest. The simpler box meaning.

The scholars cited disagree: Salonen opts for (a), Westermann opts for (b). HALOT records the disagreement without resolving it.
"Schmidt loc. cit. 69 leaves the decision open."

This is HALOT's conclusion on the Egyptian etymology: the decision is open. Not "probably Egyptian" as BDB stated. Not "Egyptian loanword" as the tradition asserted. Open. Undetermined.

This is Cohen's conclusion — "etymology undetermined" — reached independently by the field's gold standard modern lexicon, 22 years after Cohen published it. Cohen is not cited.
"In KBL both are mentioned under I תֵּבָה."

The predecessor lexicon (KBL) listed both Egyptian candidates under the first of the two proposed homonyms. HALOT notes this as historical background.

A.3 — The ANE Flood Vessel Was a Ship

"In ancient near-eastern traditional literature the vessel used by the hero of the deluge was either a huge ship, or simply a ship"

This is a remarkable statement. HALOT — the standard modern Hebrew lexicon — states explicitly that the ANE flood vessel was a ship. Not a box. Not a chest. A ship.

The Sumerian evidence: má-gur gur — a huge ship (Kramer, Pritchard). The Akkadian evidence: eleppam rabîtam — a large boat (Lambert-Millard Atra-Ḫasīs). HALOT cites the ANE parallels to establish that the flood vessel tradition is consistently nautical, not architectural.
References: N.S. Kramer Sumerian Mythology (1944; 1961²) 98; Pritchard Texts 44; Lambert-Millard Atra-Ḫasīs, The Babylonian Story of the Flood (1968) 126, line 6

Full scholarly apparatus for the ship identification. These are primary source references to the Sumerian and Babylonian flood accounts.

Section B — Additional Notes

B.1 — Ebla and Place Names

"It is uncertain whether the sbst. occurs as early as Ebla"

A possible occurrence of the word at Ebla (pre-flood era archive) is noted but flagged as uncertain. Dahood (CBQ 44, 1982) proposed it occurs in Eblaite place names ti-ba-ù and tibā-hū. HALOT treats this with appropriate caution.

B.2 — On the Origin

"on the origin of the sbst. see above A 2."

Cross-reference back to the open question. No new information — reaffirming that A.2's "decision open" is the operative conclusion.

B.3 — Later Hebrew and Related Languages

"MHeb. תֵּבָה(י) chest, cupboard, ark, (!) word"

MHeb. = Mishnaic Hebrew (post-biblical). In later Hebrew tebah came to mean chest, cupboard, ark — and also "word" (in the sense of a written word/letter). The word became generic for "container" and also developed a completely unrelated semantic field ("word") in Rabbinic usage.

The (!) signals HALOT's surprise — the same word meaning both "chest" and "word" in Mishnaic Hebrew is unusual. These are almost certainly different semantic developments of the same root, not related meanings.
DSS (Kuhn Konkordanz 230): Dam. v:1 התבה the ark

DSS = Dead Sea Scrolls. The Damascus Document (Dam.) uses tebah for the ark. Confirms the word was still in use in the Second Temple period with the Noah's ark meaning.
SamP. tîba, cstr. tîbât; JArm. תֵּיבוּתָא (Samaritan: abs. יבו; det. תיבותה) > Arb. tābūt; Armenian t'aput

The word's relatives across related languages and traditions:
SamP. = Samaritan Pentateuch — tîba
JArm. = Jewish Aramaic — tēibūtā
Arb. = Arabic — tābūt (still used in Arabic today for coffin/chest)
Armenian — t'aput (loanword)

The Arabic tābūt is particularly significant — it's a live word in modern Arabic meaning coffin or chest. This is the post-biblical semantic development: the word spread through Semitic languages with the "container" meaning. But this tells us about post-flood transmission, not pre-flood etymology.
Greek, according to Zimmern Fremdw. 45 θίβη, θήβη, θίβωνος, but on this cf. Liddell-Scott Lex. 801b, who reject θίβη as a false form.

The Greek relatives of tebah — Zimmern proposed Greek borrowed the word too, producing thibē, thēbē, thibōnos. But Liddell-Scott (LSJ) rejects thibē as a false form — not a genuine Greek word. The borrowing into Greek is therefore uncertain.

Section B.4 — The Two OT Meanings

—4) תֵּבָה in the OT: a) chest; b) casket.

HALOT's summary of the two OT usages:
a) chest — Noah's ark
b) casket — Moses' basket

Note the word choices: "chest" for Noah (large, wooden), "casket" for Moses (small, enclosed). Neither is "box" in the geometric rectangular sense. A casket is typically rounded — the word itself acknowledges the curved form of Moses' vessel.

4a — Noah's Ark

"it is the ark in the story of Noah's voyage; Sept. κιβωτός; Vulg. arca chest, box, cupboard; the word ark comes from this Latin word: Gn 6:14–9:18 (26 times)"

HALOT traces the English word "ark" explicitly to the Latin arca via Jerome's Vulgate. The transmission chain is acknowledged in the standard lexicon. The word "ark" is a Latin inheritance, not a Hebrew meaning.
"on its form, especially in connection with the voyage of Utnapištim (Epic of Gilgamesh tablet 11), see Westermann BK 1/1: 564–566, which includes the statement that in Gilgamesh 11 it is to be noted that a gigantic cubic chest is understood to be a ship, while in Gn 6 the ark, which had already been likened to a ship, is described as a box; this shows that the descriptions and the designations each have their own history."

This is the most significant statement in the entire entry. HALOT — via Westermann — makes three critical points:

1. In Gilgamesh 11 a gigantic cubic chest is understood to be a ship — the Babylonian tradition describes a box but understands it as a ship
2. In Genesis 6 the ark "had already been likened to a ship" — HALOT acknowledges the ship identification for Genesis
3. "The descriptions and the designations each have their own history" — the word used and the shape described are independent questions. The word does not determine the shape.

This is the paper's central argument stated in the standard modern Hebrew lexicon: the designation (tebah) and the description (shape) have independent histories. The word carries no geometric information.
"On the form of the ark, see e.g. von Rad ATD 2-4 (⁹1972) 95."

HALOT refers questions of the ark's shape to von Rad's commentary — acknowledging that shape is a separate question from the word's meaning.

4b — Moses' Basket

"the little casket made from papyrus that sheltered Moses in the reeds beside the Nile Ex 2:3, 5"

HALOT's gloss: "little casket made from papyrus." Note: casket, not box. Made from papyrus — the material is the primary descriptor. The word "casket" implies an enclosed container but not necessarily a rectangular one.
"Sept. accusative θῖβιν (see Liddell-Scott Lex. 801b; θῖβις or θίβις a small basket made out of strips of papyrus)"

HALOT cites LSJ's definition of thibis: "a small basket made out of strips of papyrus." Not a box. A basket. Made from strips of papyrus. The LXX's choice of thibis is confirmed by LSJ as a basket word, not a box word.

This directly contradicts the Suda's kibotion gloss — LSJ, the primary ancient Greek lexicon, says basket, not box.
"Vulg. for גֹּמֶא תֵּבַת vs.3 accusative fiscellam scirpeam a little basket made of rushes; הַתֵּבָה vs.5 accusative fiscellam."

fiscellam scirpeam = "a little basket made of rushes/bulrushes." Jerome's Vulgate — the same translation that gave us arca for Noah — used fiscella (little basket) for Moses. Jerome knew the two vessels were different objects and translated them differently. The Vulgate preserves the distinction that the tradition later collapsed.

So:
Noah's vessel: Vulgate = arca (chest/ark) → English "ark"
Moses' vessel: Vulgate = fiscella scirpea (little rush basket) → English "basket"

Jerome got Moses right. The English tradition followed him for Moses but not for Noah.
"For the bibliography (especially for 4a) see again A. Robert Fschr. 60–65; Reicke-Rost Hw. 125; IDB 1: 222ff. †"

Bibliography pointer. The † at the end = all occurrences listed, as in BDB.
