Noah Never Built an Ark

Tighter Version — Why Tebah Does Not Mean Box

The Hebrew word for Ark is tebah.

What does tebah mean?

Can't say for sure. Probably rescue vessel.

In other words: "The etymology of tebah is undetermined. In both occurrences the word functions as a functional terminus technicus describing a vessel whose purpose is the preservation of life."

Cohen, C. "Hebrew tbh: Proposed Etymologies." JANES 4 (1972): 37–51.

Introduction

The Hebrew word tebah (תֵּבָה) occurs exactly twice in the Old Testament — once for Noah's vessel (Genesis 6–9) and once for Moses' basket (Exodus 2:3,5). The box-shaped Ark assumption — that tebah specifically denotes a rectangular vessel — dominates both popular and scholarly literature. This paper examines every argument for the box reading and finds that none survive scrutiny. The case for the box is weaker than its proponents acknowledge. The case against it is stronger than its critics have noticed.

The argument is presented in two parts. Part I states the case for the box reading at its strongest — eighteen arguments drawn from etymology, translation tradition, scholarly assertion, parallel literature, and creationist engineering. Part II dismantles each argument in turn. The reader is invited to find any argument in Part I that Part II does not adequately answer.

Part I — The Case for the Box

The following eighteen arguments are drawn from the published literature and stated at their strongest. No rebuttal is offered here.

From Etymology

  1. Tebah is probably an Egyptian loanword from ḏbꜣ.t — meaning chest or coffin 1,2,3.
  2. Egyptian ḏbꜣ.t denotes a box-shaped object — chest, coffer, sarcophagus 1.
  3. The consonantal similarity t-b / d-b suggests a phonetic connection between the Hebrew and Egyptian words 1,3.

From Cassuto

  1. Tebah "precisely" means parallelepiped — a rectangular solid 4.
  2. Modern Hebrew uses tebah as the mathematical term for parallelepiped/cuboid — confirming the geometric meaning 4,5.
  3. The Ark cannot rest on the mountains of Ararat if ship-shaped — a box can rest on flat ground; a ship cannot 4.
  4. The Ark moves by wind and water, not by sailors — therefore it is not a ship 4.

From the Translation Tradition

  1. The LXX translates tebah as kibotos — chest or box — confirming the geometric reading 6.
  2. Jerome translates tebah as arca — chest or box 7.
  3. Every major English translation uses "ark" — a word implying a rectangular vessel 8,9.

From the Babylonian Parallel

  1. The Babylonian flood vessel (Gilgamesh XI) is described as cubic — the Genesis vessel may share the same tradition 10.
  2. Haupt (1927) discusses the Babylonian vessel as box-shaped — Cassuto uses this to support the parallelepiped claim 4,10.

From Modern Usage

  1. Modern Hebrew tebah = geometric cuboid/parallelepiped — the mathematical term confirms the ancient meaning 5.

From Creationist Literature

  1. "The Hebrew word itself implies this" box shape 11.
  2. A box maximises volume for animal capacity calculations — the practical choice for the Ark's purpose 12.
  3. Hong et al.'s proportions study assumes a box hull and finds the Genesis dimensions near-optimal 13.
  4. A box is the simplest construction for ancient builders — the natural default.

From Scholarly Consensus

  1. Wenham (1987), Hoffmeier (1997), Spoelstra (2020) — all accept the box reading 14,15,16.

Part II — The Demolition

Each argument is addressed in turn. Where an argument fails on multiple grounds, all grounds are given.

Arguments 1–3 — The Egyptian Etymology

The proposed equation fails on four independent grounds:

(a) No motive. Linguistic borrowing follows a simple rule: languages borrow words for concepts they lack. Hebrew did not lack a word for chest, box, or coffin. Aron (אָרוֹן) covers all three — 200+ occurrences. Moses used it for the Ark of the Covenant (Exodus 25) and for Joseph's coffin in Egypt (Genesis 50:26). Nobody borrows a word for a concept their language already expresses perfectly well. The proposed borrowing has no motive before step one is reached 17.

(b) Semantic mismatch. Egyptian ḏbꜣ.t is never used for boats in Egyptian texts 17. The proposed donor word denotes shrine, sarcophagus, or chest — objects that sit on land and house the dead. Neither occurrence of tebah describes a static land-based funerary object.

(c) Phonetic mismatch. The consonantal correspondence is: Egyptian d/t → Hebrew t (arguable); Egyptian b → Hebrew b (certain); Egyptian t (feminine ending) → Hebrew h (mismatch). One certain match, one arguable, one mismatch. Not a valid cognate by modern phonological standards 17.

(d) Silent retraction by the equation's own author. Erman accepted Brugsch's equation in 1892 2. In 1926 Erman co-authored the authoritative Wörterbuch der Ägyptischen Sprache 18 — the equation is absent. Nobody noticed for 46 years. Cohen noticed in 1972 17. Lambdin's specialist catalogue 19 also omits tebah — an independent silent rejection. BDB's own qualifier "probable" 3 signals uncertainty the tradition subsequently hardened to certainty.

All four failures are independent. The proposed etymology fails comprehensively at every level of evaluation.

Arguments 4–7 — Cassuto's Claims

Argument 4 — "Precisely a parallelepiped" 4: Cassuto's footnote 236 cites only Haupt (1927) 10 — a paper about the Babylonian vessel, not Hebrew etymology. No philological source is cited for the geometric meaning of tebah. Assertion dressed as conclusion.

Argument 5 — Modern Hebrew usage 4,5: Confirmed 5 — but catastrophically circular. The modern mathematical term almost certainly derives from the box-reading tradition under examination. LXX imposes box (c.250 BC) → 1,500 years of box tradition → modern Hebrew adopts tebah for cuboid → Cassuto cites modern Hebrew as independent confirmation. The tradition is citing itself as its own source.

Argument 6 — Cannot rest on flat ground 4: At approximately 40,000 tonnes displacement, modern bulk carriers and VLCCs all rest on flat bottoms in dry dock routinely. Scale changes everything. A small-boat intuition applied to a large-vessel specification.

Argument 7 — Not navigated therefore not a ship 4: A vessel is defined by hull form and function, not navigation. Cohen demolishes this in footnotes 10–11 17 — Hebrew hālak is used for ships (Is. 33:21; Ps. 104:26) and Akkadian alāku is standard for ship movement including passive drift.

Genesis 7:18 turns the same argument back. The verse uses וַתֵּלֶךְ — from hālak — with the Ark as subject: the Ark went upon the face of the waters. Not "floated" (buoyancy) but "went" (translation). The narrative environment is מַבּוּל — a term used almost exclusively of Noah's Flood in the entire Hebrew Bible, denoting overwhelming, world-engulfing, dynamic waters. The Ark traveled on those waters for five months under a divine wind (Genesis 8:1) and came to rest in Ararat — a different location from departure. Travel on dynamic floodwaters without crew and without navigation is precisely the engineering environment that requires passive directional stability — weathervaning. A box hull cannot weathervane. The AiG Ark Encounter hull achieves it automatically through hull form alone: bow freeboard asymmetry + stern keels + tapered profile. No crew, no sail, no ballast trim required.

Arguments 8–10 — The Translation Tradition

The LXX 6 does not consistently translate tebah as kibotos. For Noah: kibotos. For Moses' basket: thibis 20 — "a small basket made out of strips of papyrus" (Liddell-Scott 20). The same Hebrew word, two different Greek translations. Kibotos is also used for the Ark of the Covenant — nobody argues it is box-shaped on the basis of the word.

The English word "ark" has no Hebrew ancestry 8,9. The chain: kibotos 6 → arca 7 → "ark" 8. Three translations, no Hebrew. Tyndale 9 saw tebah and still wrote "ark" — the word was already fixed.

Arguments 11–12 — The Babylonian Parallel

Westermann 21: "the descriptions and the designations each have their own history." HALOT 22 cites this directly. The Atrahasis flood vessel: eleppam rabîtam 23 — a large ship. The Sumerian flood vessel: a huge ship 24. The Babylonian cubic dimensions are numerologically motivated — the sacred iku unit — not hydrodynamically determined. The Babylonian parallel does not support the box reading — it undermines it.

Argument 13 — Modern Hebrew Usage

Addressed under Argument 5. Circular 5.

Arguments 14–17 — Creationist Literature

Argument 14 — Morris (1971) 11: "The Hebrew word itself implies this" — no citation. Cohen published one year later 17. The failure belongs to post-1972 authors who repeated the claim after Cohen.

Argument 15 — Volume calculations 12: True but irrelevant to the meaning of tebah. Using a box for calculations does not establish that tebah means box.

Argument 16 — Hong et al. 13: A proportions study, not a hull form study. Hong states explicitly: "Since all hull forms in this study had a rectangular cross section, the GZ curve could be determined analytically." All 13 comparative hulls were rectangular throughout — Cb ≈ 0.95–0.98. The AiG Ark Encounter at Cb = 0.87 was never in Hong's comparison set. The difference is approximately 7% overall — but that 7% is entirely concentrated in the bow (Cb = 0.594) and stern (Cb = 0.656), precisely where directional stability is generated. Hong confirmed near-optimal proportions for rectangular hulls. He never tested the hull form that passively weathervanes. The box assumption was inherited, not established.

Argument 17 — Simplest construction: Correct — but every large timber ocean-going vessel in the archaeological and iconographic record has a curved hull 25,26,27. Phoenician, Minoan, Greek, Roman, Viking, Chinese, Indian, Polynesian — four thousand years, every independent tradition, unanimously rejected the easy box. The box is the carpenter's default. Every shipbuilder rejected it.

Argument 18 — Scholarly Consensus

Wenham 14 cites Cassuto and adds nothing. Hoffmeier 15 asserts Egyptian etymology without engaging Cohen 17. Spoelstra 16 cites Cohen six times, engages his central argument zero times. BDB embedded "probable" 3 in 1906. The tradition copied the conclusion and dropped the qualifier. Probable became certain without a single additional piece of evidence.

The Positive Case — What Tebah Does Mean

After eliminating every proposed geometric meaning, what remains is Cohen's conclusion: a functional descriptor with no geometric content. The word describes purpose, not form. Both occurrences share one property — the preservation of life — and nothing else. Scale differs (300 cubits vs infant-sized). Material differs (unknown wood vs papyrus). Crew differs (eight people vs no crew). Duration differs (one year vs hours). Setting differs (open ocean vs river reeds). What is shared: a vessel preserves life in circumstances where death is the alternative.

The Targum Onkelos — the authoritative Aramaic translation of the Torah — renders tebah as תֵּיבוּ. This is not a translation. It is the same word in the sister dialect — same root ת-ב, same consonants, different morphological ending. The Aramaic speakers imposed no geometry because they needed no interpretation. They already had the word. The communities linguistically closest to the Hebrew original understood tebah without geometric imposition. The box required Greek librarians in Alexandria to invent it and German philologists in the 1860s to explain it. Everyone else was fine.

HALOT (1999) — the gold standard modern Hebrew lexicon, the replacement for BDB — concludes: "decision open." It also states, via Westermann: "the vessel used by the hero of the deluge was a huge ship, or simply a ship." The authoritative modern lexicon independently reaches Cohen's conclusion via a different route — the ANE cuneiform scholarship tradition rather than Hebrew philology. Two independent lines of evidence, no communication between them, same answer.

The Engineering Consequence

Genesis 6:15 provides dimensions: 300×50×30 cubits. These are principal dimensions — they specify the maximum extent of the vessel in three axes. They do not specify cross-sectional shape. A naval architect requires approximately ten parameters to define a hull: length, beam, depth, draft, block coefficient (Cb), prismatic coefficient (Cp), midship coefficient (Cm), waterplane coefficient (Cwp), bow geometry, and stern geometry. Genesis provides three of these ten. It says nothing about the remaining seven. Genesis specifies the Ark at the level of a container — an enclosing volume. The hull lives inside that space, shaped by engineering requirements the text neither specifies nor constrains. The box reading treats a container specification as a hull specification. They are different documents. Genesis wrote one of them.

Neither "just a barge" nor "a conventional ship." A purpose-built vessel in the transitional region between the two — with enough hull form to weathervane in the מַבּוּל, and enough cargo volume to carry every land animal kind through a year-long voyage.

No large timber ocean-going vessel in the archaeological or iconographic record has a box hull. Every independent ancient shipbuilding tradition — Phoenician, Minoan, Greek, Roman, Viking, Chinese, Indian, Polynesian — chose curved hulls for open water across four thousand years of documented history. The box is the carpenter's default. Every timber shipbuilder rejected it. The Ark is specified as a timber vessel (ăṣê-gōper). The relevant comparison set is timber vessels. The engineering verdict of the entire ancient world is against the box.

The AiG Ark Encounter — ship-shaped, 157 metres, this paper's author as design consultant — is the physical result of taking Cohen seriously. The word carries no shape. The dimensions specify an envelope. Naval architecture fills the envelope with the hull form that satisfies every engineering constraint the flood voyage imposes.

Conclusion

Eighteen arguments for the box reading. None survive scrutiny. The etymology fails on four independent grounds. Cassuto's geometric assertion is unreferenced, its modern Hebrew evidence is circular, and its two engineering arguments are demonstrably wrong. The translation tradition is internally inconsistent — the LXX used two different words for the same Hebrew word in two adjacent books. The Babylonian parallel has an independent history from Genesis. Modern Hebrew usage is circular. The creationist literature inherited the box without checking the Hebrew. Scholarly consensus is transmission by copying.

The process of elimination confirms the conclusion independently: every proposed geometric meaning fails for Moses' basket, and Hebrew had a better word for every proposed meaning that Moses demonstrably knew and chose not to use.

What remains: a functional descriptor with no geometric content. A vessel word defined entirely by purpose. Both occurrences preserve life. The etymology is undetermined. The shape is not in the word.

Tebah means rescue vessel.
