Noah's Ark — Naval Architecture Research

Noah Never Built an Ark

Tighter Version — Why Tebah Does Not Mean Box

T. Lovett | Draft | 2026 | Full paper →

The Hebrew word for Ark is tebah.

What does tebah mean?

Can't say for sure. Probably rescue vessel.

In other words: "The etymology of tebah is undetermined. In both occurrences the word functions as a functional terminus technicus describing a vessel whose purpose is the preservation of life."

Cohen, C. "Hebrew tbh: Proposed Etymologies." JANES 4 (1972): 37–51.

Tebah (תֵּבָה) occurs 26 times for Noah's vessel (Genesis 6–9) and twice for Moses' basket (Exodus 2:3, 2:5). It is never used for any other object in any Hebrew text. This paper examines every argument for the box reading and finds that none survive scrutiny.

The Egyptian Etymology

Brugsch (1867) proposed that tebah derives from Egyptian ḏbꜣ.t — chest, coffer, sarcophagus.1,2 Erman (1892) accepted this and BDB (1906) embedded it: "prob. Egypt. loan-word from t-b-t, chest, coffin."3 The equation has been copied ever since.

It fails on four independent grounds. First, Hebrew had no motive to borrow — aron (אָרוֹן, 200+ occurrences) already covered chest, box, and coffin; Moses used it for the Ark of the Covenant and for Joseph's coffin in Egypt.17 Second, Egyptian ḏbꜣ.t is never used for boats in Egyptian texts — it denotes land-based funerary objects.17 Third, the consonantal correspondence gives one certain match, one arguable, and one mismatch — not a valid cognate by modern phonological standards.17 Fourth, Erman himself silently withdrew the equation: he accepted it in 1892 but omitted it from his authoritative Wörterbuch der Ägyptischen Sprache in 1926.18 Nobody noticed for 46 years. Lambdin's specialist Egyptian loanword catalogue (1953) independently omits tebah.19 Cohen (1972) documented all of this in a peer-reviewed paper that has never been rebutted.17 BDB's own qualifier — "probably" — was dropped by every tradition that copied it.

Cassuto's Parallelepiped Claim

Cassuto (1964) asserted that tebah "precisely" means parallelepiped — a rectangular solid — and that "undoubtedly the Biblical narrative refers to such a structural shape and not to that of a ship."4 He cited modern Hebrew mathematical usage of tebah for cuboid as confirmation.5

No philological source is cited for the geometric meaning. Cassuto's footnote 236 references only Haupt (1927) — a paper about the Babylonian vessel, not Hebrew etymology.10 "Precisely" signals confidence in the absence of derivation. The modern Hebrew confirmation is catastrophically circular: the mathematical term tebah = cuboid almost certainly entered Hebrew vocabulary because of the box-reading tradition — the very tradition Cassuto is trying to confirm. The LXX imposed box in 250 BC; 1,500 years of box tradition followed; modern Hebrew adopted tebah for cuboid from that tradition; Cassuto cited the result as independent evidence. The tradition is citing itself.

Cassuto also argued that a ship-shaped Ark could not rest on the mountains of Ararat — a box rests on flat ground, a ship cannot.4 At approximately 40,000 tonnes displacement, modern bulk carriers and VLCCs rest on flat bottoms in dry dock routinely. Scale changes everything. He further argued that since the Ark moved by wind and water rather than sailors, it is not a ship.4 Cohen demolished this in footnotes 10–11: Hebrew hālak is used for ships (Is. 33:21; Ps. 104:26) and Akkadian alāku is standard for ship movement including passive drift.17 Genesis 7:18 uses וַתֵּלֶךְ — the Ark went upon the face of the waters of the מַבּוּל — overwhelming, world-engulfing, dynamic floodwaters. It traveled for five months under a divine wind and came to rest in Ararat. That is not floating in place. Travel without crew on dynamic seas requires passive directional stability — weathervaning. A box hull cannot weathervane.

The Translation Tradition

The LXX (c.250 BC) rendered tebah as kibotos — chest or box — for Noah's vessel.6 Jerome (c.400 AD) translated it as arca.7 Wycliffe (1382) coined "ark" from arca.8 Tyndale (1530) went back to the Hebrew, saw tebah, and still wrote "ark" — the English word was already fixed.9 The English word "ark" has no Hebrew ancestry. It is Greek → Latin → English only.

The LXX does not consistently translate tebah as kibotos. For Moses' basket it used thibis — defined by Liddell-Scott as "a small basket made out of strips of papyrus."20 The same Hebrew word, two different Greek translations. The Alexandrian translators themselves recognised that Noah's vessel and Moses' basket could not share one geometric word — an implicit acknowledgement that tebah carries no consistent geometric meaning. Kibotos is also used for the Ark of the Covenant — nobody argues it is box-shaped on the basis of the word. The translation tradition confirms the box reading only if its internal inconsistency is ignored.

The Babylonian Parallel

The Babylonian flood vessel (Gilgamesh XI) is described as cubic.10 Cassuto cited Haupt (1927) to suggest Genesis and Gilgamesh share a common cubic conception.4 Westermann (1974) addressed this directly: "the descriptions and the designations each have their own history."21 HALOT (1999) cites this passage and concludes the flood vessel "was either a huge ship, or simply a ship."22 The Atrahasis flood vessel is eleppam rabîtam — a large ship.23 The Sumerian flood vessel is a huge ship.24 The Babylonian cubic dimensions are numerologically motivated — the sacred iku unit — not hydrodynamically derived. The parallel undermines the box reading rather than supporting it.

The Biblical Text

Genesis 6:14–16 describes rooms, three levels, a door, and a light source — architectural language applied to a vessel's interior. A ship also has rooms, levels, a door, and a light source. The absence of nautical vocabulary does not imply a box hull — it implies a specification written for a non-specialist audience. Genesis provides three of the ten parameters a naval architect needs to define a hull: length, beam, and depth. It says nothing about draft, block coefficient, bow geometry, stern geometry, or any hull form descriptor. Three dimensions specify a bounding envelope — not a hull. The container specification is not the hull specification. Genesis wrote one of them.

Engineering

Whitcomb and Morris (1961) used a box hull for animal capacity calculations — a box maximises volume for given principal dimensions.12 True, but irrelevant to the meaning of tebah. Using a box for calculations does not establish that tebah means box. Hong et al. (1994) conducted a seakeeping study at KRISO finding the Genesis proportions near-optimal — using a box hull throughout.13 Hong states explicitly: "all hull forms in this study had a rectangular cross section." All 13 comparative hulls were rectangular — Cb ≈ 0.95–0.98. The AiG Ark Encounter at Cb = 0.87 was never in Hong's comparison set. The 7% Cb difference is entirely concentrated in the bow (Cb = 0.594) and stern (Cb = 0.656) — precisely where directional stability is generated. Hong confirmed near-optimal proportions for rectangular hulls. He never tested the hull form that passively weathervanes.

Since timber planks are naturally straight, a box is the easiest large timber structure to build. Correct — but every large timber ocean-going vessel in the archaeological record has a curved hull. Phoenician, Minoan, Greek, Roman, Viking, Chinese, Indian, Polynesian — four thousand years of independent traditions unanimously rejected the easy box for open water. The box is the carpenter's default. Every shipbuilder rejected it.

Scholarly Consensus

Morris (1971) asserted "the Hebrew word itself implies this" box shape — no citation, no philological source.11 Cohen published one year later.17 Wenham (1987) cites Cassuto and adds nothing.14 Hoffmeier (1997) asserts Egyptian etymology without engaging Cohen.15 Spoelstra (2020) cites Cohen six times and engages his central argument zero times.16 Scholarly consensus in this case is transmission by copying. BDB embedded "probable" in 1906; the tradition copied the conclusion and dropped the qualifier.

What Tebah Does Mean

The Targum Onkelos renders tebah as Aramaic תֵּיבוּ — not a translation but the same word in the sister dialect, same root ת-ב, same consonants, different morphological ending.29 The Aramaic speakers imposed no geometry because they needed no interpretation. They already had the word. The box required Greek librarians in Alexandria to invent it and German philologists in the 1860s to explain it.

HALOT (1999) — the gold standard modern Hebrew lexicon, replacement for BDB — concludes: "decision open"; "the vessel used by the hero of the deluge was either a huge ship, or simply a ship."22 Two independent lines — Cohen's Hebrew philology and Westermann's ANE cuneiform scholarship — reach the same conclusion without citing each other.

The Engineering Consequence

The block coefficient of the AiG Ark Encounter hull has been calculated from the definitive CAD geometry file (AR-003-20110418, Parasolid format) sent to Troyer Architects on 18 April 2011, using displaced volume below the 15-cubit waterline (Genesis 7:20): Cb = 0.87. Full hydrostatic data at hull-form.

With Cb = 0.87 the Ark lies at the upper end of full-bodied ship forms — squarely in Great Lakes bulk carrier territory. Ocean-going barges have Cb of 0.92 and above. A naval architect seeing only "length ~157m, Cb 0.87" would think "very full ship" before thinking "ocean barge."

Naval architect Jim King (Creation Museum, 2005) proposed Great Lakes bulk carriers as the nearest modern analogue — independently confirmed by the CAD calculation. Neither "just a barge" nor "a conventional ship" — a purpose-built vessel in the transitional region between the two, with enough hull form to weathervane in the מַבּוּל and enough cargo volume to carry every land animal kind through a year-long voyage.

The AiG Ark Encounter — ship-shaped, 157 metres, this paper's author as design consultant — is the physical result of taking Cohen seriously. The word carries no shape. The dimensions specify an envelope. Naval architecture fills the envelope with the hull form that satisfies every engineering constraint the flood voyage imposes.

Conclusion

The Egyptian etymology fails on four independent grounds. Cassuto's geometric assertion is unreferenced, its modern Hebrew evidence is circular, and its two engineering arguments are demonstrably wrong. The translation tradition is internally inconsistent. The Babylonian parallel has its own independent history from Genesis. The biblical text specifies a container, not a hull. The creationist literature inherited the box without checking the Hebrew. Scholarly consensus is transmission by copying.

Tebah fails every proposed geometric meaning for Moses' basket, and Hebrew had a better word for every proposed meaning that Moses demonstrably knew and chose not to use.

What remains: a functional descriptor with no geometric content. A vessel word defined entirely by purpose. The etymology is undetermined. The shape is not in the word.

Tebah means rescue vessel.
