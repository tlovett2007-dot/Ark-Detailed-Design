Noah's Ark — Naval Architecture Research

Noah Never Built an Ark

Why Tebah Does Not Mean Box

T. Lovett | Draft — abbreviated version | 2026 | Full paper with references →

The Hebrew word for Ark is tebah.

What does tebah mean?

Can't say for sure. Probably rescue vessel.

In other words: "The etymology of tebah is undetermined. In both occurrences the word functions as a functional terminus technicus describing a vessel whose purpose is the preservation of life."

Cohen, C. "Hebrew tbh: Proposed Etymologies." JANES 4 (1972): 37–51.

Introduction

The Hebrew word tebah (תֵּבָה) occurs exactly twice in the Old Testament — once for Noah's vessel (Genesis 6–9) and once for Moses' basket (Exodus 2:3,5). The box-shaped Ark assumption — that tebah specifically denotes a rectangular vessel — dominates both popular and scholarly literature. This paper examines every argument for the box reading and finds that none survive scrutiny. The case for the box is weaker than its proponents acknowledge. The case against it is stronger than its critics have noticed.

The argument is presented in two parts. Part I states the case for the box reading at its strongest — eighteen arguments drawn from etymology, translation tradition, scholarly assertion, parallel literature, and creationist engineering. Part II dismantles each argument in turn. The reader is invited to find any argument in Part I that Part II does not adequately answer.

Part I — The Case for the Box

The following eighteen arguments are drawn from the published literature and stated at their strongest. No rebuttal is offered here.

From Etymology

  1. Tebah is probably an Egyptian loanword from ḏbꜣ.t — meaning chest or coffin (Brugsch 1867; Erman 1892; BDB 1906).
  2. Egyptian ḏbꜣ.t denotes a box-shaped object — chest, coffer, sarcophagus (Brugsch).
  3. The consonantal similarity t-b / d-b suggests a phonetic connection between the Hebrew and Egyptian words (Brugsch).

From Cassuto

  1. Tebah "precisely" means parallelepiped — a rectangular solid (Cassuto 1964, fn. 236).
  2. Modern Hebrew uses tebah as the mathematical term for parallelepiped/cuboid — confirming the geometric meaning (Cassuto).
  3. The Ark cannot rest on the mountains of Ararat if ship-shaped — a box can rest on flat ground; a ship cannot (Cassuto).
  4. The Ark moves by wind and water, not by sailors — therefore it is not a ship (Cassuto).

From the Translation Tradition

  1. The LXX translates tebah as kibotos — chest or box — confirming the geometric reading (LXX c.250 BC).
  2. Jerome translates tebah as arca — chest or box (Vulgate c.400 AD).
  3. Every major English translation uses "ark" — a word implying a rectangular vessel (KJV tradition).

From the Babylonian Parallel

  1. The Babylonian flood vessel (Gilgamesh XI) is described as cubic — the Genesis vessel may share the same tradition (popular argument).
  2. Haupt (1927) discusses the Babylonian vessel as box-shaped — Cassuto uses this to support the parallelepiped claim (Cassuto fn. 236).

From Modern Usage

  1. Modern Hebrew tebah = geometric cuboid/parallelepiped — the mathematical term confirms the ancient meaning (modern Hebrew dictionaries).

From Creationist Literature

  1. "The Hebrew word itself implies this" box shape (Morris 1971, CRSQ 8:142).
  2. A box maximises volume for animal capacity calculations — the practical choice for the Ark's purpose (Whitcomb and Morris 1961).
  3. Hong et al.'s proportions study assumes a box hull and finds the Genesis dimensions near-optimal — confirming the box (Hong et al. 1994).
  4. A box is the simplest construction for ancient builders — the natural default (popular creationist argument).

From Scholarly Consensus

  1. Wenham (1987), Hoffmeier (1997), Spoelstra (2020) — all accept the box reading. Scholarly consensus confirms it.

Part II — The Demolition

Each argument is addressed in turn. Where an argument fails on multiple grounds, all grounds are given.

Arguments 1–3 — The Egyptian Etymology

The proposed equation fails on four independent grounds:

(a) No motive. Linguistic borrowing follows a simple rule: languages borrow words for concepts they lack. Hebrew did not lack a word for chest, box, or coffin. Aron (אָרוֹן) covers all three — 200+ occurrences. Moses used it for the Ark of the Covenant (Exodus 25) and for Joseph's coffin in Egypt (Genesis 50:26). Nobody borrows a word for a concept their language already expresses perfectly well. The proposed borrowing has no motive before step one is reached.

(b) Semantic mismatch. Egyptian ḏbꜣ.t is never used for boats in Egyptian texts (Cohen 1972, fn. 14). The proposed donor word denotes shrine, sarcophagus, or chest — objects that sit on land and house the dead. Neither occurrence of tebah describes a static land-based funerary object. The semantic fields do not overlap at the relevant point.

(c) Phonetic mismatch. The consonantal correspondence is: Egyptian d/t → Hebrew t (arguable — voiced/unvoiced pair); Egyptian b → Hebrew b (certain); Egyptian t (feminine ending) → Hebrew h (mismatch — Egyptian feminine t does not regularly produce Hebrew he). One certain match, one arguable, one mismatch. This is not a valid cognate by modern phonological standards.

(d) Silent retraction by the equation's own author. Erman accepted Brugsch's equation in 1892 (ZDMG 46:123). In 1926 Erman co-authored the authoritative Wörterbuch der Ägyptischen Sprache — the definitive Egyptian dictionary. The equation is absent. Erman omitted his own accepted equation from his own masterwork. Nobody noticed for 46 years. Cohen noticed in 1972 (fn. 14, via Prof. J. Schmidt, Columbia Egyptologist). Lambdin's specialist catalogue of Egyptian loanwords in Hebrew (1953) also omits tebah — an independent silent rejection. BDB's own qualifier "probable" signals uncertainty the tradition subsequently hardened to certainty.

The equation fails at the motive stage independently of arguments b, c, and d. All four failures are independent. The proposed etymology is not a marginal case requiring fine judgment — it fails comprehensively at every level of evaluation.

Arguments 4–7 — Cassuto's Claims

Argument 4 — "Precisely a parallelepiped": Cassuto's footnote 236 cites only Haupt (1927) — a paper about the Babylonian vessel, not Hebrew etymology. No philological source is cited for the geometric meaning of tebah. The word "precisely" signals confidence in the absence of derivation. This is assertion dressed as conclusion.

Argument 5 — Modern Hebrew usage: Modern Hebrew does use tebah (תיבה) as the mathematical term for cuboid — confirmed by the Hebrew Wikipedia article on the subject. However this confirmation is catastrophically circular. The modern mathematical term almost certainly entered Hebrew vocabulary because of the box-reading tradition — the very tradition Cassuto is trying to confirm. The sequence: LXX imposes box (c.250 BC) → 1,500 years of box tradition → modern Hebrew adopts tebah for cuboid → Cassuto cites modern Hebrew as independent confirmation. The tradition is citing itself as its own source. This is not evidence.

Argument 6 — Cannot rest on flat ground: Cassuto's mental model is a small keeled sailing vessel. At the scale of Noah's vessel — 300×50×30 cubits, approximately 40,000 tonnes displacement — the engineering drives in the opposite direction. Modern bulk carriers, tankers, and VLCCs all rest on flat bottoms in dry dock routinely. Flat-bottomed vessels at large scale are the norm, not the exception. Scale changes everything. This is not an engineering argument — it is a small-boat intuition applied to a large-vessel specification.

Argument 7 — Not navigated therefore not a ship: A vessel is defined by its hull form and function, not by whether it is navigated. An uncrewed ship is still a ship. A disabled supertanker drifting in a storm is still a ship. Cohen demolishes this argument in footnotes 10–11, demonstrating that the Hebrew verb hālak is used elsewhere for ships (Is. 33:21; Ps. 104:26) and that Akkadian alāku is the standard term for ship movement including passive drift.

Arguments 8–10 — The Translation Tradition

The LXX does not consistently translate tebah as kibotos. For Noah's vessel: kibotos. For Moses' basket: thibis (θῖβις) — a different Greek word entirely, defined by Liddell-Scott as "a small basket made out of strips of papyrus." The same Hebrew word, two different Greek translations. The Alexandrian translators themselves recognised that Noah's vessel and Moses' basket could not both be described by one geometric word. Their solution was to use two different Greek words — which is an implicit acknowledgement that tebah cannot carry consistent geometric meaning across both occurrences.

Kibotos is also used for the Ark of the Covenant — an object nobody argues is box-shaped on the basis of the word. The word is functional, not geometric.

The English word "ark" has no Hebrew ancestry. The chain is: kibotos (Greek) → arca (Latin, Jerome c.400 AD) → "ark" (English, Wycliffe 1382). Three translations, no Hebrew. Tyndale (1530) went back to the Hebrew, saw tebah, and still wrote "ark" — the word was already fixed in English vocabulary. The box tradition in English derives from a Greek translation choice made in Alexandria c.250 BC, not from the Hebrew text.

Arguments 11–12 — The Babylonian Parallel

Westermann (1974) addresses the Gilgamesh parallel explicitly: "in Gilgamesh 11 it is to be noted that a gigantic cubic chest is understood to be a ship, while in Gn 6 the ark, which had already been likened to a ship, is described as a box; this shows that the descriptions and the designations each have their own history" (BK 1/1:564–566). HALOT (1999) cites this passage directly. The Babylonian cubic dimensions are numerologically motivated — the sacred Babylonian iku unit — not hydrodynamically determined. The Genesis and Gilgamesh traditions have independent histories. The Babylonian cubic description does not determine the meaning of Hebrew tebah.

Argument 13 — Modern Hebrew Usage

Addressed under Argument 5 above. Circular.

Arguments 14–17 — Creationist Literature

Argument 14 — Morris (1971): "The Hebrew word itself implies this" — no citation, no philological source, no lexicon referenced. Cohen's demolition of every proposed etymology was published one year later. Morris inherited the BDB consensus and stated it in a parenthesis. The failure belongs to post-1972 creationist authors who repeated the claim after Cohen published.

Argument 15 — Volume calculations: A box maximises volume for a given set of principal dimensions. This is true — and irrelevant to the meaning of tebah. Whitcomb and Morris used a box for calculation convenience, not because tebah means box. Hong et al. (1994) did the same. The proportions study is valid regardless of hull form at Cb≈0.85 — a ship-shaped hull with the same principal dimensions inherits Hong's proportions result. Using a box for calculations does not establish that tebah means box.

Argument 16 — Hong et al.: Hong's study is a proportions study, not a hull form study. It tests 13 barge-type variants — all with near-rectangular cross-sections. It does not test ship-shaped hulls. Its conclusion — that the Genesis proportions are near-optimal — is valid for ship-shaped hulls at Cb≈0.85 where principal dimensions dominate seakeeping behaviour. The box assumption was inherited, not established.

Argument 17 — Simplest construction: Timber planks want to be straight. A box from timber is indeed simpler than a curved hull — which requires steam bending, clamping, and skilled craftsmanship. This is correct. But every large timber ocean-going vessel in the archaeological and iconographic record has a curved hull — Phoenician, Minoan, Greek, Roman, Viking, Chinese, Indian, Polynesian. Four thousand years of timber shipbuilding, every independent tradition, unanimously rejected the easy box in favour of the difficult curve — because open water demanded it. The box is the carpenter's default. Every shipbuilder rejected it.

Argument 18 — Scholarly Consensus

Wenham (1987) cites Cassuto and accepts his conclusion — adding nothing. Hoffmeier (1997) asserts Egyptian etymology without engaging Cohen. Spoelstra (2020) cites Cohen six times and engages his central argument zero times. Scholarly consensus in this case is transmission by copying, not confirmation by independent analysis. BDB embedded "probable" in 1906. The tradition copied the conclusion and dropped the qualifier. "Probable" became "certain" without a single additional piece of evidence being adduced.

The Process of Elimination

Independently of the demolition above, tebah can be eliminated from every proposed geometric or categorical meaning by two simple tests: (1) does the meaning work for both occurrences? (2) did Hebrew already have a better word?

The Positive Case — What Tebah Does Mean

After eliminating every proposed geometric meaning, what remains is Cohen's conclusion: a functional descriptor with no geometric content. The word describes purpose, not form. Both occurrences share one property — the preservation of life — and nothing else. Scale differs (300 cubits vs infant-sized). Material differs (unknown wood vs papyrus). Crew differs (eight people vs no crew). Duration differs (one year vs hours). Setting differs (open ocean vs river reeds). What is shared: a vessel preserves life in circumstances where death is the alternative.

The Targum Onkelos — the authoritative Aramaic translation of the Torah — renders tebah as תֵּיבוּ. This is not a translation. It is the same word in the sister dialect — same root ת-ב, same consonants, different morphological ending. The Aramaic speakers imposed no geometry because they needed no interpretation. They already had the word. The communities linguistically closest to the Hebrew original understood tebah without geometric imposition. The box required Greek librarians in Alexandria to invent it and German philologists in the 1860s to explain it. Everyone else was fine.

HALOT (1999) — the gold standard modern Hebrew lexicon, the replacement for BDB — concludes: "decision open." It also states, via Westermann: "the vessel used by the hero of the deluge was a huge ship, or simply a ship." The authoritative modern lexicon independently reaches Cohen's conclusion via a different route — the ANE cuneiform scholarship tradition rather than Hebrew philology. Two independent lines of evidence, no communication between them, same answer.

The Engineering Consequence

Genesis 6:15 provides dimensions: 300×50×30 cubits. These are principal dimensions — they specify the maximum extent of the vessel in three axes. They do not specify cross-sectional shape. A naval architect reading 300×50×30 sees a design space, not a blueprint. Hull form is determined by engineering requirements: stability, seakeeping, structural efficiency, and wave behaviour.

No large timber ocean-going vessel in the archaeological or iconographic record has a box hull. Every independent ancient shipbuilding tradition — Phoenician, Minoan, Greek, Roman, Viking, Chinese, Indian, Polynesian — chose curved hulls for open water across four thousand years of documented history. The box is the carpenter's default. Every timber shipbuilder rejected it. The Ark is specified as a timber vessel (ăṣê-gōper). The relevant comparison set is timber vessels. The engineering verdict of the entire ancient world is against the box.

The AiG Ark Encounter — ship-shaped, 157 metres, this paper's author as design consultant — is the physical result of taking Cohen seriously. The word carries no shape. The dimensions specify an envelope. Naval architecture fills the envelope with the hull form that satisfies every engineering constraint the flood voyage imposes.

Conclusion

Eighteen arguments for the box reading. None survive scrutiny. The etymology fails on four independent grounds. Cassuto's geometric assertion is unreferenced, its modern Hebrew evidence is circular, and its two engineering arguments are demonstrably wrong. The translation tradition is internally inconsistent — the LXX used two different words for the same Hebrew word in two adjacent books. The Babylonian parallel has an independent history from Genesis. Modern Hebrew usage is circular. The creationist literature inherited the box without checking the Hebrew. Scholarly consensus is transmission by copying.

The process of elimination confirms the conclusion independently: every proposed geometric meaning fails for Moses' basket, and Hebrew had a better word for every proposed meaning that Moses demonstrably knew and chose not to use.

What remains: a functional descriptor with no geometric content. A vessel word defined entirely by purpose. Both occurrences preserve life. The etymology is undetermined. The shape is not in the word.

Tebah means rescue vessel.
