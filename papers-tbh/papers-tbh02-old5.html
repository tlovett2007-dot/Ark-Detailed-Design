Noah's Ark — Naval Architecture Research

Noah Never Built an Ark

Tighter Version — Why Tebah Does Not Mean Box

T. Lovett | Draft | 2026 | Full paper →

The Hebrew word for Ark is tebah.

What does tebah mean?

Can't say for sure. Probably rescue vessel.

In other words: "The etymology of tebah is undetermined. In both occurrences the word functions as a functional terminus technicus describing a vessel whose purpose is the preservation of life."

Cohen, C. "Hebrew tbh: Proposed Etymologies." JANES 4 (1972): 37–51.

Tebah (תֵּבָה) occurs 26 times for Noah's vessel (Genesis 6–9) and twice for Moses' basket (Exodus 2:3, 2:5). It is never used for any other object in any Hebrew text — just the ark and the basket. This paper examines common arguments for the box reading.

The Egyptian Loanword

Brugsch (1867) proposed that tebah derives from Egyptian ḏbꜣ.t — chest, coffer, sarcophagus.1,2 Erman (1892) accepted this and BDB (1906) embedded it: "prob. Egypt. loan-word from t-b-t, chest, coffin."3 Equating tebah with ḏbꜣ.t has been copied ever since.

Etymology lacks a motive. Why loan a word when you already have one? Hebrew had aron (אָרוֹן, 202 occurrences) to describe a covered chest, a box, or a coffin; Moses used it for the Ark of the Covenant and for Joseph's coffin in Egypt.4 The Egyptian word ḏbꜣ.t is never used for boats in Egyptian texts — it only ever denotes land-based funerary objects,5 yet Noah survived in a big floating object, Moses in a little floating object, both the same word. Phonology has no match. The idea of matching the letters of the תֵּבָה and ḏbꜣ.t ( or TBH and DBT) is weak. Of the three consonants, one was a match (B), one arguable (T is moved), and one a mismatch (H and D)— not a valid cognate by modern phonological standards. Not that you need to be a philologist to find it unconvincing that two words in different languages are "probably" the same word if they share the same middle letter. 6 Egyptology has lost support. The Egyptologist Erman did take notice. He had initially agreed with Brugsch in 1892, but later changed his mind, omitting the equation from his authoritative Wörterbuch der Ägyptischen Sprache in 1926.7 Nobody seemed to catch on until 1953 when tebah was also ignored in Lambdin's specialist Egyptian loanword catalogue.8 The equation was losing support among those best placed to evaluate it. Semantics says tevah is more likely a life preserver. Cohen (1972) documented all this in a peer-reviewed paper on the etymology of tebah, a paper that has never been rebutted. Cohen reiterated to me in 2004 that his paper has been cited many times and that he still stood by what he said back in 1972. 910 BDB's own qualifier — "probably" — was eventually dropped by every tradition that copied it. HALOT has now lowered it to "open". Archaeology says ancient Egyptian basketry usually takes round or oval forms, produced naturally by the dominant coil construction technique. 11 We can't use a coffin to imply a box shape here when Moses' baby basket was almost certainly rounded, like nearly every Egyptian coil-formed common basket. Logically it makes no sense to even use the word for "coffin": It may seem like a good idea to have Moses use an Egyptian word since he grew up in Egypt, but a "coffin"? Why would he say his mother put him in a coffin to die in front of his sister? Similarly, how is "coffin" a suitable word to describe a colossal vessel full of animals and provisions for the express purpose of keeping the occupants alive, and important enough to keep God waiting (1 Peter 3:20)? The Egyptian loanword proposed in 1867 finds no support from etymology, phonology, Egyptology, semantics, archaeology, or even logic.

Cassuto (1964) went further, asserting that tebah "precisely" means parallelepiped — a rectangular solid — and that "undoubtedly the Biblical narrative refers to such a structural shape and not to that of a ship."12 He cited modern Hebrew mathematical usage of tebah for cuboid as confirmation.13 No philological source is cited for the geometric meaning. Cassuto's footnote 236 references only Haupt (1927) — a paper about the Babylonian vessel, not Hebrew etymology.14 "Precisely" signals confidence in the absence of derivation. The modern Hebrew confirmation is circular: the mathematical term tebah = cuboid almost certainly entered Hebrew vocabulary from the box-reading tradition — the very tradition Cassuto is trying to confirm. The tradition is citing itself.

The Translation Tradition

The LXX (c.250 BC) rendered tebah as kibotos — chest or box — for Noah's vessel.18 Jerome (c.400 AD) translated it as arca.19 Wycliffe (1382) coined "ark" from arca.20 Tyndale (1530) went back to the Hebrew, saw tebah, and still wrote "ark" — the English word was already fixed.21 The English word "ark" has no Hebrew ancestry. It is Greek → Latin → English only.

The LXX does not consistently translate tebah as kibotos. For Moses' basket it used thibis — defined by Liddell-Scott as "a small basket made out of strips of papyrus."22 The same Hebrew word, two different Greek translations. The Alexandrian translators themselves recognised that Noah's vessel and Moses' basket could not share one geometric word — an implicit acknowledgement that tebah carries no consistent geometric meaning. Kibotos is also used for the Ark of the Covenant — nobody argues it is box-shaped on the basis of the word. The translation tradition confirms the box reading only if its internal inconsistency is ignored.

The Babylonian Parallel

The Babylonian flood vessel (Gilgamesh XI) is described as cubic.23 Cassuto cited Haupt (1927) to suggest Genesis and Gilgamesh share a common cubic conception.24 Westermann (1974) addressed this directly: "the descriptions and the designations each have their own history."25 HALOT (1999) cites this passage and concludes the flood vessel "was either a huge ship, or simply a ship."26 The Atrahasis flood vessel is eleppam rabîtam — a large ship.27 The Sumerian flood vessel is a huge ship.28 The Babylonian cubic dimensions are numerologically motivated — the sacred iku unit — not hydrodynamically derived. The parallel undermines the box reading rather than supporting it.

Three Dimensions is most likely a Box

Genesis 6:15 gives three numbers: 300 cubits long, 50 cubits wide, 30 cubits high. The most literal interpretation is presumed to be a box.

Compare the Titanic: 269 metres long, 28 metres wide, and 18 metres deep to Noah's Ark: was 157 metres long, 26 metres wide, and 16 metres deep. Which is bigger? The Titanic of course. When you compared those two sets of dimensions you never thought: "I never knew the Titanic was a box". Three dimensions communicate scale and proportion; they say nothing about hull form.

It is consistent to recognise the Genesis account is highly abbreviated. It took (***) words to describe a temporary portable temple in the desert, yet only (***) words to describe a massive vessel that saved the world's fauna. Genesis 6:14–22 does not mention the number of animals. Does Noah just guess and start building nests with no idea how many animals are coming? What is "lower, second and third" are they decks or what? We have to fill in the words with the most obvious logical conclusion - decks. And they fit really well in the 30 cubit height. How much food, and why is water totally missing? We are not given a full specification. This emphasises that we can't say something did not exist simply because it is not mentioned. This is a logical fallacy called the argument from silence (argumentum ex silentio). Take water as an obvious example. God instructs Noah: "take with you every sort of food that is eaten" (Genesis 6:21). The Hebrew word for food in Genesis 6:21 — מַאֲכָל — specifically means solid food that is eaten. Water has its own Hebrew word, מַיִם, used hundreds of times in the Old Testament. Nobody thinks Noah took no water because it only says "food". Yet "water" is much easier to explain than hull form. Silence on hull form does not mean it is more likely a formless box, just as Noah was not more likely to have no water.

The Biblical Text

Genesis 6:14–16 describes rooms, three levels, a door, and a light source — architectural language applied to a vessel's interior. A ship also has rooms, levels, a door, and a light source. The absence of nautical vocabulary does not imply a box hull — it implies a specification written for a non-specialist audience. Genesis provides three of the ten parameters a naval architect needs to define a hull: length, beam, and depth. It says nothing about draft, block coefficient, bow geometry, stern geometry, or any hull form descriptor. Three dimensions specify a bounding envelope — not a hull. The container specification is not the hull specification. Genesis wrote one of them.

Engineering prefers a modified box

Cassuto argued that a ship-shaped Ark could not rest on the mountains of Ararat — a box rests on flat ground, a ship cannot.15 At approximately 40,000 tonnes displacement, modern bulk carriers and VLCCs rest on flat bottoms in dry dock routinely. Scale changes everything. He further argued that since the Ark moved by wind and water rather than sailors, it is not a ship.16 Cohen demolished this in footnotes 10–11: Hebrew hālak is used for ships (Is. 33:21; Ps. 104:26) and Akkadian alāku is standard for ship movement including passive drift.17 Genesis 7:18 uses וַתֵּלֶךְ — the Ark went upon the face of the waters of the מַבּוּל — overwhelming, world-engulfing, dynamic floodwaters. It traveled for five months under a divine wind and came to rest in Ararat. That is not floating in place. Travel without crew on dynamic seas requires passive directional stability — weathervaning. A box hull cannot weathervane.

Whitcomb and Morris (1961) used a box hull for animal capacity calculations — a box maximises volume for given principal dimensions.29 So he chose the smallest cubit to be minimise space and then a perfect box to maximise space. Using a box for calculations does not establish that tebah means box. Hong et al. (1994) conducted a seakeeping study at KRISO finding the Genesis proportions near-optimal — using a box-like hull throughout.30 Hong states explicitly: "all hull forms in this study had a rectangular cross section." All 13 comparative hulls were rectangular — Cb ≈ 0.95–0.98. (ref this to explain what Cb means) The AiG Ark Encounter at Cb = 0.87 is less box-like than Hong's comparison set, putting it between a full-bodied ship and a barge. (note about Jim King choosing a laker for hull form) Lowering Cb by 7% reduces accelerations from waves and improves directional stability in wind-generated waves (Gen 8:1). A hidden clue in the Hong study is that shorter hulls were better than Noah's Ark in a confused sea (ref what that means, and the final results), leading to the obvious question: While the proportions of Noah's Ark are rather good at handling a random sea state, it is a bit too long. Is there an additional requirement to more comfortably handle a wind-driven regular sea state while resisting getting trapped side-on to the waves in a beam sea?

Since timber planks are naturally straight, a box is the easiest large timber structure to build. Correct — but every large timber ocean-going vessel in the archaeological record has a curved hull. Phoenician, Minoan, Greek, Roman, Viking, Chinese, Indian, Polynesian — thousands of years of independent traditions unanimously rejected the box shape for open water. The box is the carpenter's default, but every shipbuilder rejected it. So for Noah to build a ship in the shape of a box looks more like an igorant farmer than a competent shipwright at 500 years of age.

Scholarly Consensus

Morris (1971) asserted "the Hebrew word itself implies this" box shape — no citation, no philological source.31 Cohen published one year later.32 Wenham (1987) cites Cassuto and adds nothing.33 Hoffmeier (1997) asserts Egyptian etymology without engaging Cohen.34 Spoelstra (2020) cites Cohen six times and engages his central argument zero times.35 Scholarly consensus in this case is transmission by copying. BDB embedded "probable" in 1906; the tradition copied the conclusion and dropped the qualifier.

The Process of Elimination

Tebah fails every proposed geometric meaning on two independent tests: does the meaning work for both occurrences, and did Hebrew already have a better word?

What Tebah Does Mean

The Targum Onkelos renders tebah as Aramaic תֵּיבוּ — not a translation but the same word in the sister dialect, same root ת-ב, same consonants, different morphological ending.36 The Aramaic speakers imposed no geometry because they needed no interpretation. They already had the word. The box required Greek librarians in Alexandria to invent it and German philologists in the 1860s to explain it.

HALOT (1999) — the gold standard modern Hebrew lexicon, replacement for BDB — concludes: "decision open"; "the vessel used by the hero of the deluge was either a huge ship, or simply a ship."37 Two independent lines — Cohen's Hebrew philology and Westermann's ANE cuneiform scholarship — reach the same conclusion without citing each other.

The Engineering Consequence

The block coefficient of the AiG Ark Encounter hull has been calculated from the definitive CAD geometry file (AR-003-20110418, Parasolid format) sent to Troyer Architects on 18 April 2011, using displaced volume below the 15-cubit waterline (Genesis 7:20): Cb = 0.87. Full hydrostatic data at hull-form.

With Cb = 0.87 the Ark lies at the upper end of full-bodied ship forms — squarely in Great Lakes bulk carrier territory. Ocean-going barges have Cb of 0.92 and above. A naval architect seeing only "length ~157m, Cb 0.87" would think "very full ship" before thinking "ocean barge."

Naval architect Jim King (Creation Museum, 2005) proposed Great Lakes bulk carriers as the nearest modern analogue — independently confirmed by the CAD calculation. Neither "just a barge" nor "a conventional ship" — a purpose-built vessel in the transitional region between the two, with enough hull form to weathervane in the מַבּוּל and enough cargo volume to carry every land animal kind through a year-long voyage.

The AiG Ark Encounter — ship-shaped, 157 metres, this paper's author as design consultant — is the physical result of taking Cohen seriously. The word carries no shape. The dimensions specify an envelope. Naval architecture fills the envelope with the hull form that satisfies every engineering constraint the flood voyage imposes.

Conclusion

The Egyptian etymology fails on four independent grounds. Cassuto's geometric assertion is unreferenced, its modern Hebrew evidence is circular, and its two engineering arguments are demonstrably wrong. The translation tradition is internally inconsistent. The Babylonian parallel has its own independent history from Genesis. The biblical text specifies a container, not a hull. The creationist literature inherited the box without checking the Hebrew. Scholarly consensus is transmission by copying.

Tebah fails every proposed geometric meaning for Moses' basket, and Hebrew had a better word for every proposed meaning that Moses demonstrably knew and chose not to use.

What remains: a functional descriptor with no geometric content. A vessel word defined entirely by purpose. The etymology is undetermined. The shape is not in the word.

Tebah means rescue vessel.
