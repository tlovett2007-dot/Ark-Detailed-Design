The case for a box

Summary of Arguments

The best arguments for Anoh's Ark being a box shape:

Argument Strength Comments
No propulsion ⇒ no hydrodynamic requirement Moderate Genesis gives the Ark no means of propulsion, steering or navigation. Once movement through the water is no longer a design objective, one of the principal reasons for adopting a streamlined hull disappears. This shifts the design optimum toward a fuller, more box-like form, although it does not require one.
Maximum cargo efficiency Weak–moderate Genesis defines the Ark as a container for preserving living creatures. A box maximises usable floor area and enclosed volume for a given overall size, providing the most efficient arrangement for animals, aisles and stores. Conventional ships also seek cargo capacity, but sacrifice usable space to satisfy hydrodynamic requirements.
Ease of construction Very weak A rectangular structure can be built from repetitive straight members with simpler joinery than a curved hull. However, ancient shipwrights were fully capable of constructing sophisticated curved vessels, so this is only a minor supporting argument.
Babylonian flood vessel precedents Moderate The two principal Babylonian flood traditions both describe vessels that depart radically from conventional ship design. The traditional interpretation of the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic is a cube (or cuboid), while the Ark Tablet describes a giant circular coracle. Although they differ in geometry, both are purpose-built survival structures rather than practical seagoing ships. This demonstrates that Babylonian flood tradition accepted highly geometric vessel forms, making a box-like Genesis Ark culturally plausible.
Greek reinterpretation of tebah Weak The Septuagint translators replaced the unique Hebrew tebah with κιβωτός (kibōtos), an ordinary Greek word for a box, chest or ark. This may indicate that the translators understood tebah as denoting a container rather than a ship, although it is an interpretation made centuries after Genesis was written.
Babylonian flood vessel precedents Weak If tebah ultimately derives from an Egyptian word associated with a chest, box or coffin—as proposed by several scholars before Cohen's critique—this would favour a box-like concept. However, because no Egyptian etymology has achieved scholarly consensus and Cohen argues that the evidence is insufficient, this line of argument remains tentative.
