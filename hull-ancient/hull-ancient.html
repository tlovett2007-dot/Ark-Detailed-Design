The Core Structural Question

The structural question is not whether planks were built before frames or frames before planks — it is whether the plank connections transfer shear. This is the test that separates a structural shell from a mere covering.

Shear transfer between planks is what makes the shell act as a unified membrane — carrying longitudinal load, distributing transverse pressure, and resisting racking. Without it, the planking is merely a weather skin and the frames carry everything. At small scale this is adequate. At 150m scale it fails.

Build sequence is a red herring. Viking clinker is plank-first but only partially shear-continuous — the lapped riveted planks transfer some shear but not efficiently. The WW1 diagonal-planked motor ships are rib-first but fully shear-continuous — the geometry enforces shear transfer regardless of fastener type. The Chinese multi-layer crossed carvel achieves moderate shear continuity through geometric interlock between layers, not through individual plank connections.

What matters is whether shear transfers between planks. That is the structural question. Build sequence is merely the method of achieving it — or failing to.

The Structural Problem at Scale

The shear-continuity question only becomes critical at large scale. Small boats tolerate non-continuous shells because longitudinal bending loads are modest and the frame can carry them. As vessels grow, the hull acts as a beam spanning between wave crests — hogging and sagging — and the frame-as-spine model fails.

ScaleFrame-dominant performanceShear-continuous shell advantage
Small (under 20m)Adequate — longitudinal bending loads modestMinimal — extra effort not justified

Large (over 100m)Fails — keel cannot carry longitudinal bendingEssential — only structurally credible solution

The Northern European tradition demonstrates what happens when shipbuilders develop independently from small boats: they invented rib-first, caulked-carvel construction — non-shear-continuous — and used it successfully at small scale. As vessels grew, the structural inadequacy became apparent. Each incremental scale increase seemed manageable until suddenly it wasn't. The Wyoming (329ft, 1909) is the canonical failure — structural steel keelsons introduced to compensate created stiffness mismatch, concentrated loads at connection points, and the hull worked and leaked until it foundered.

The Mediterranean tradition started with mortise and tenon shell construction — shear-continuous from the beginning. The most parsimonious explanation is that it was transmitted from a context where the correct answer was already required: a vessel large enough that non-shear-continuous construction was not an option.

The Shear Continuity Spectrum in Practice

Viking Clinker — Partially Shear-Continuous

Viking ships were built plank-first — the shell erected before internal frames were inserted. But the lapped and riveted clinker planks transfer shear only partially through the lap joint. The resulting shell is stiffer than non-continuous carvel but not a fully stressed skin. Viking shipbuilders exploited this deliberately — the semi-flexible shell absorbed wave energy by working slightly, which suited following-sea conditions in the North Atlantic. At small-to-medium scale this works well. At large scale the flexibility becomes hogging.

Chinese Multi-Layer Planking — Geometric Shear Continuity

Chinese shipbuilders developed a multi-layer planking system — up to seven layers documented on large junks — with pitched fabric between layers and staggered lap geometry between successive layers. The staggering is the key: the outer layer planks are offset so their edges fall mid-span on the inner layer planks, like brickwork bonding. An inner plank cannot slide longitudinally without dragging the outer plank edge with it — geometric interlock without any fastener carrying shear.

The pitched fabric between layers serves two simultaneous functions: waterproofing across the layer interface, and flexible gasket accommodating differential shrinkage and swelling between layers. The hull becomes progressively tighter in service as the timber swells and the pitch flows into micro-gaps.

The Chinese also used this system for hull life extension — adding new outer layers over ageing inner layers rather than rebuilding. Each new layer restores structural integrity and waterproofing while the inner layers remain as structural backup. The practical result is a hull that improves with age rather than deteriorating.

This is not plank-first construction and not mortise-and-tenon construction — it is an independent Chinese development of geometric shear continuity through layered staggered planking. It achieves moderate shear transfer without any plank-to-plank mechanical connection. The engineering insight is the same as the ancient Mediterranean tradition; the method is completely different.

Diagonal Planking — Full Geometric Shear Continuity

The most structurally complete timber solution is diagonal planking — two layers of planking applied at opposing angles (typically ±45° to the hull centreline). The crossing geometry means any shear load in one layer is directly resisted by the perpendicular fibres of the other layer. Shear transfer is complete regardless of how the planks are fastened — the geometry enforces it.

Diagonal planking over erected frames (rib-first construction) appears in late 19th century English vessels and was developed extensively in WW1 US motor ships. It was then largely superseded by steel — not because it failed structurally but because steel was cheaper and faster. Mine hunters kept diagonal timber construction alive through the 1990s because timber is non-magnetic.

Diagonal planking is the timber equivalent of modern steel plate construction — a fully isotropic shell carrying loads in all directions. It is rib-first and fully shear-continuous simultaneously, which demonstrates that build sequence is irrelevant to the structural outcome.

The Mortise and Tenon Evidence

Ancient Mediterranean shipbuilders — Greeks, Phoenicians, and earlier Aegean cultures — achieved shear continuity through mortise and tenon edge joinery. A rectangular tenon cut into one plank edge fits a matching mortise in the adjacent plank. The shell was built first; frames were inserted afterward to maintain form. The joinery locks the planks together mechanically — shear transfers through the tenon.

Underwater archaeology has recovered numerous ancient Mediterranean vessels with this joinery. The density of joints is striking — in many cases extreme, with joints so close that the net timber section between tenons is critically reduced. This over-specification is archaeologically significant: it suggests the practice was transmitted without the engineering theory. Builders knew to join the planks; they did not know the optimum spacing from shear flow analysis; they defaulted to more joints as the safe assumption.

The correct answer — optimum joint spacing where joint capacity and net section capacity are simultaneously satisfied — requires knowledge of shear flow in the shell. That knowledge had not been transmitted with the technique. The over-jointed hulls are the archaeological signature of transmitted practice without transmitted theory.

Edge-Drilled Dowels — Noah's Proposed Method

For Noah's Ark, edge-drilled dowels are proposed in preference to mortise and tenon. The drill enters from the plank edge, parallel to the face, locking adjacent planks together — the dowel entirely hidden within the joint. This technique is historically attested in 19th century American riverboat construction, independently developed under practical construction pressure.

The round dowel removes more material than a rectangular mortise — the net section reduction requires that joint spacing be determined by stress analysis rather than intuition. Noah, with Tubal-Cain's metalworking tradition (Genesis 4:22) providing quality auger bits and engineering competence, would have had both the tooling and the analytical framework to determine correct spacing. The Greek transmission via mortise and tenon may reflect adaptation to available tooling after Babel dispersion.

WW1 — Independent Rediscovery

World War 1 steel shortages in the US forced a return to large timber cargo ship construction. The resulting vessels show the empirical solution reached under material pressure: heavy deck strapping, thick sheer strakes, strong deck beams, modest keel scantlings. The shell carrying the longitudinal load. The deck carrying the tension in hogging. The keel reduced to a skid.

These builders did not have stressed skin theory. They had performance pressure and scarce material. The correct answer emerged from necessity — the same answer the ancient Mediterranean tradition had reached empirically, and the same answer Noah's engineering situation demanded from the beginning.
