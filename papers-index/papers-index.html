Paper 1 — Hong Revisited: A Technical Audit of Hong et al. (1994)

ElementDetail
StatusQuickest to write — all source material in hand
Target journalJournal of Creation / Answers Research Journal
TopicTechnical audit of the foundational KRISO seakeeping study — identifies five limitations, demonstrates each understates Ark performance

Hong et al. (1994) is the foundational peer-reviewed study on Noah's Ark seakeeping and structural safety. Nine KRISO naval architects confirmed feasibility using professional methodology. However the study contains five significant limitations, each of which makes the result more conservative than the actual vessel warrants:

#LimitationEffect on result
1Timber modelled as membrane elements (planking) and truss elements (frames) — steel FEA methodology applied without adjustmentUnderstates bending stiffness and composite action of stressed-skin timber construction — actual structure stiffer and stronger
2Confused sea assumption — waves from all directions equally probableIgnores weathervaning advantage — organised following sea is significantly less severe than confused sea of same Hs
3Box hull (Cb ≈ 1.0) — all 13 comparative hulls rectangular cross-sectionShip-shaped hull never tested — curved bilge, deadrise, and flare would improve seakeeping and structural performance
4Roll modelling explicitly acknowledged as unreliable — "strong nonlinear behaviour due to viscous damping"Roll analysis understated — keel damping not quantified; actual roll behaviour better than modelled
5Transcription error in English translation Table 3 — AR and Safety Index columnsCorrected values derived from Hong's own data; average absolute error 0.1% — does not affect conclusions but requires correction for the record

Conclusion: Hong et al. confirmed Ark feasibility using conservative assumptions and an inadequate material model. The actual vessel — stressed-skin timber construction, weathervaning in organised seas, ship-shaped hull — outperforms Hong's model on every metric. The study understates the case it was making.

This paper requires no new research — all material is in hand. The transcription error is already documented in Lovett (2024). The membrane/truss critique follows directly from standard FEA methodology. Estimated writing time: shortest of all candidate papers.

Paper 2 — Noah Never Built an Ark: Why Tebah Does Not Mean "Box"

ElementDetail
StatusIn development — see working draft
Target journalJournal of Creation / Answers Research Journal
TopicHebrew word study — tebah carries no geometric information; box-Ark assumption enters through Latin arca, not Hebrew

The Hebrew word tebah (תֵּבָה) appears only twice in Scripture — Noah's vessel (Genesis 6–9) and Moses' basket (Exodus 2:3). Cohen (1972) demolished the Egyptian loanword etymology in peer-reviewed philological research; the field has cited his paper for 53 years without engaging his conclusions. Cassuto's "precisely parallelepiped" claim is an unreferenced assertion in a commentary. Lambdin's (1953) specialist Egyptian loanword catalogue omits tebah entirely. Spoelstra (2020), the most recent dedicated treatment, confirms the functional reading but inconsistently accepts the box shape while citing Cohen six times and engaging him zero times. The box-Ark assumption is a transmission error: tebah → kibotos → arca (Jerome) → "Ark" (English). The shape of Noah's vessel comes from naval architecture, not from the word used to describe it.

Paper 3 — How Many Animals Fit? A Volume-First Engineering Analysis

ElementDetail
StatusMethodology established — see working draft
Target journalAnswers Research Journal (Georgia Purdom contact)
TopicVolume-first methodology — inverts all prior literature; engineering upper bound on kind count; convergence with AiG baraminology

All prior feasibility studies go animals→volume: count kinds, show they fit, declare feasibility. This paper inverts the methodology — starting from Genesis dimensions, deducting all non-animal volumes systematically (structure, services, ballast, fodder, family quarters), and deriving animal capacity as the residual. Current best estimate of effective animal zone: ~11,107 m³ (~17.7% of external volume), yielding ~1,300–1,700 kinds at 80% utilisation.

The key finding is convergence: AiG baraminology independently estimates ~1,400 kinds from taxonomic analysis. Two independent methods — engineering and taxonomy — bracket the same number. This is a non-trivial result. The paper's primary contribution is the methodology and the convergence finding.

Paper 4 — What Was Gopher Wood? The Argument from Silence

ElementDetail
StatusEarly development — see working draft
Target journalJournal of Creation / Answers Research Journal
TopicGopher (גֹּפֶר) — single occurrence, Genesis 6:14. No cognates, no identification in 2,500 years of scholarship. The silence is evidence.

Gopher wood is a hapax legomenon — one occurrence in the entire Hebrew Bible. Unlike every common ANE timber, which has a well-attested Hebrew name, gopher has resisted identification since the LXX translators encountered it in 250 BC. The LXX retreat to "squared timbers" (a processing description, not a species name) suggests the word had already lost its referent within 2,000 years of the flood. The extinction hypothesis — the flood itself eliminated the species — explains the persistent failure of identification. Naval architecture constraints provide a property profile: large-dimension, flexible, rot-resistant, resinous timber. A full transmission table of proposed identifications is in development.

Writing Guidelines

These guidelines apply to all papers in this programme. The methodology is as important as the argument.

1. The Model — Chayim Cohen (1972)

The model for this paper series is Chayim Cohen, "Hebrew tbh: Proposed Etymologies," JANES 4 (1972): 37–51.

Cohen understood tebah better than anyone before or since. He wrote it in plain declarative sentences. Fifteen pages — approximately seven pages of argument, fifty percent citations. No thesaurus required. No conclusion buried under the apparatus of scholarship. When he was done, he walked away.

Fifty-four years later it remains the definitive treatment. The papers in this series attempt the same discipline.

2. Plain Language

The best writers use the easiest words to build something both convincing and succinct. Complexity in writing signals one of two things: the author doesn't fully understand what they're saying, or they're trying to impress rather than communicate.

Specific prohibitions:

AvoidBecauseInstead
Thesaurus substitutionsObscures meaning; signals insecurityUse the word you thought of first
Passive constructions where active is possibleDistances author from argument"Cohen demolishes this" not "this has been demolished"
Hedged clauses inside hedged clausesBuries the pointState the point; hedge once if necessary
RepetitionSignals lack of confidence in the argument or distrust of the readerSay it once, clearly, move on
Academic throat-clearing"It is perhaps not without interest to note that...""Note that..."

3. Length

Cohen: seven pages of argument, fifty percent citations. If the argument needs more than seven pages of prose, it is not sharp enough yet. Every sentence must be load-bearing. Nothing decorative.

The test: could this sentence be cut without losing anything? If yes, cut it.

4. Titles — Cohen's Method

Cohen titled his paper "Hebrew tbh: Proposed Etymologies." He stated the subject. He did not explain the answer. The reader opens the paper to find out.

Good titles state the question or the subject. They do not summarise the conclusion.

WeakStrongWhy
"Why Tebah Does Not Mean Box and What This Means for Ark Design""Noah Never Built an Ark"States the provocation; reader must open to understand
"Passive Weathervaning: The Engineering Case for the Genesis Proportions""Noah's Ark: If It Only Had to Float, Why Is It So Long?"States the puzzle; answer withheld
"Antediluvian Vocabulary and Why Post-Flood Etymology Searches Fail""The Words That Didn't Survive the Flood"Vivid, precise, no explanation needed

5. The Pitchfork Method

Cohen's argument structure:

  1. Survey all proposed solutions
  2. Test each against the actual evidence
  3. Demolish each on its own terms — the pitchfork through the straw
  4. Acknowledge honestly what remains: unknown
  5. Then — and only then — propose the completely different direction

The critical move is step 4. "Unknown" is not a publishable conclusion and scholars feel pressure to fill the void. Resist it. An honest "unknown" is more valuable than a confident wrong answer. BDB filled the void with Egyptian db't in 1906. The field has been paying for it ever since.

A dead end properly documented is load-bearing. It tells the reader the question was asked, the evidence was checked, and the answer is no — and here is why.

6. Honest Concession

Give ground where ground is due. The strongest arguments acknowledge what the opposition gets right before demolishing what it gets wrong.

Example: a squat vessel — even one approaching cubic proportions — can be made survivable with careful management of centre of gravity and metacentric height. This was correctly noted by naval architect Jim King at the first serious creationist technical discussion of the Ark design in 2005. King was right. Acknowledging this makes the subsequent argument — that the cube cannot weathervane — land harder, not softer.

Rhetorical overreach is the enemy of credibility. If the argument is sound it does not need exaggeration.

7. Vivid Language — Footnotes Are the Safe Zone

A memorable phrase is worth more than a paragraph of careful hedging. But main text and footnotes differ in register:

LocationRegisterExample
Main textPlain, precise, restrained"The equation is, as Cohen noted, conspicuously absent from Lambdin's authoritative catalogue."
FootnotesSlightly more latitude — Cohen put his sharpest observations in footnotes"The consonantal match between Hebrew tebah and Egyptian db't shares some consonants — if you squint. Egyptologists have not been persuaded."

The footnote test: would a sharp reviewer object to this in the main text? If yes, move it to a footnote. If yes even there, cut it from the paper — but keep it for the book.

8. Structure

Every section advances. No section revisits ground already covered. The argument is a forward march, not a spiral.

If the outline has more than eight sections, the argument has not been sharpened enough yet.

9. The Three-Import Problem — A Case Study in Method

The tebah paper illustrates the pitchfork method applied to a word that has been boxed in from three directions simultaneously:

Import vectorSourceBox wordProblem
Egyptian loanwordBrugsch (1867) → Erman (1892) → BDB (1906)db't — chest, coffinNever used for boats in any Egyptian text; consonantal match requires squinting; demolished by Cohen (1972)
LXX translationAlexandria c.250 BCkibotos — chest, boxTranslators imported their Covenant Ark associations into tebah; the box assumption may originate here, not in the Hebrew
Akkadian cognateVarioustubbû — containerContested; same pattern — a box word from another language retrofitted to confirm a conclusion already reached

Three linguistic traditions. Three box words. The conclusion preceded the evidence in every case. The "evidence" was retrofitted to confirm a box assumption that arrived from outside the Hebrew text. Tebah in Hebrew carries none of it.

The pitchfork method requires demolishing all three vectors independently before proposing the completely different direction. This is the structure of the tebah paper.

10. The Music Analogy

A great mix has space. Every element audible. Nothing competing. The worst mixes are the ones where the producer added everything because they weren't confident enough to take anything away.

The argument is strong enough — it does not need dressing up. Trust the reader.

Less is more. Cohen proved it in 1972. Fifteen pages. Walk away.
