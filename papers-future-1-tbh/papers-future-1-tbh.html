Title: Noah Never Built an Ark: Why Tebah Does Not Mean "Box"

Target journal: Journal of Creation / Answers Research Journal

Argument Summary

The following points constitute the core argument. Each links to its full development below.

  1. Tebah appears only twice in Scripture — Noah's vessel and Moses' basket. No other occurrences exist from which to derive geometric meaning.
  2. Moses' basket cannot be rectangular — woven papyrus reed construction is physically incapable of producing sharp corners. If tebah means box, Moses' mother wove a rectangular box from bulrushes. Absurd.
  3. The shape argument proves too much — if tebah carries the shape of Moses' basket, Noah's Ark was round. Nobody accepts this, which demonstrates the argument's invalidity.
  4. The LXX does not consistently translate tebah as kibotos — it uses kibotos (κιβωτός) for Noah but thibis (θῖβις) for Moses. The translators themselves recognised the two objects could not be the same geometric shape.
  5. Kibotos is functional, not geometric — it also describes the Ark of the Covenant, which nobody argues is box-shaped on the basis of this word. Kibotos means "protective container," not "rectangular box."
  6. Cassuto's parallelepiped claim is unreferenced assertion — the only scholarly source for the geometric reading of tebah is a commentary footnote with no philological derivation. Cohen (1972) demolishes it with targeted peer-reviewed research.
  7. Cohen's conclusion — tebah is a functional descriptor: a life-preserving vessel. It carries no geometric information.
  8. Spoelstra (2020) — most recent dedicated treatment. Confirms tebah is a terminus technicus for a life-preserving receptacle. Useful ally, but inconsistently accepts Cassuto's box shape from the dimensions without rebutting Cohen's refutation of Cassuto.
  9. The positive case — shape comes exclusively from Genesis 6:15 (300×50×30 cubits). These are dimensions, not a cross-section. A naval architect reads them as an envelope specifying size, not shape. The shape is determined by naval architecture, not by the word tebah.
  10. The English word "Ark" is the culprit — from Latin arca (chest). The box-shape assumption entered the translation tradition through the English word, not through the Hebrew original.

1. Tebah Appears Only Twice

Tebah (תֵּבָה) occurs exactly twice in the entire Hebrew Bible:

ReferenceObjectMaterialShape given by text
Genesis 6–9 — Noah's vesselSeagoing vesselGopher wood300×50×30 cubits (dimensions only)
Exodus 2:3 — Moses' basketInfant's basketBulrushes (papyrus reed)None given

With only two occurrences and no other attestations, the word's meaning must be derived from etymology and context — not from accumulated usage. This severely limits any claim about geometric content.

2. Moses' Basket Cannot Be Rectangular

Papyrus reed construction produces curved vessels by the nature of the material. Reeds are flexible and taper — they cannot be formed into sharp-cornered rectangular geometry without a rigid framework that the text does not mention and ancient practice does not support. Moses' basket was round or oval, as the material demands.

If tebah means "rectangular box," Moses' mother wove a rectangular box from bulrushes. This is physically impossible. Therefore tebah cannot mean "rectangular box." QED.

Spoelstra recognises this implicitly — his Egyptian loanword thesis (*ḏbȝ.t* = coffer/coffin/*Götterschrein*) attempts to explain the functional parallel (life-preservation) while sidestepping the geometric impossibility. But a coffin-shaped papyrus basket is no more possible than a box-shaped one. The material constraint is absolute.

3. The Shape Argument Proves Too Much

If tebah carries the geometric character of Moses' basket — which is round or oval — then Noah's Ark was round. This is the Irving/coracle position, and nobody accepts it as consistent with the explicit Genesis dimensions.

An argument that produces a conclusion universally rejected demonstrates its own invalidity. The shape argument from tebah therefore defeats itself regardless of which direction it is pushed — toward box (physically impossible for Moses) or toward round (absurd for Noah). The word carries no geometric information in either direction.

4. The LXX Does Not Consistently Translate Tebah

The standard box-Ark argument runs: tebah → kibotos (LXX) → chest/box (English). But the LXX translators did not treat the two tebah occurrences as the same word:

ReferenceHebrewLXXMeaning
Genesis 6 — Noah's vesseltebahkibotos (κιβωτός)Chest, protective container
Exodus 2:3 — Moses' baskettebahthibis (θῖβις)Egyptian rush-basket — specifically a woven reed vessel

The LXX translators used thibis for Moses — an Egyptian loanword specifically denoting a woven rush basket — because they recognised that a reed basket could not be a kibotos. This is an implicit acknowledgement that tebah carries no fixed geometric meaning. The translators supplied the geometry from context, not from the Hebrew word itself.

Spoelstra notes this split but does not draw the full implication: if the LXX translators — who had access to the original Hebrew and its cultural context — did not treat the two tebah occurrences as geometrically equivalent, the box-Ark argument from kibotos collapses at its first step.

5. Kibotos Is Functional, Not Geometric

Kibotos (κιβωτός) in the LXX and NT describes:

ObjectReference
Noah's vesselGenesis 6–9 (LXX); Matthew 24:38; Hebrews 11:7; 1 Peter 3:20
Ark of the CovenantExodus 25 (LXX); Hebrews 9:4; Revelation 11:19
Chests and boxes (general)Various

Nobody argues the Ark of the Covenant's shape from the word kibotos. Its shape is known from the explicit construction specifications of Exodus 25 — not from the word used to describe it. The same principle applies to Noah's vessel: shape comes from the explicit dimensions of Genesis 6:15, not from kibotos or tebah.

Kibotos describes function — a protective container that preserves its contents. It is not a geometric term.

6. Cassuto's Parallelepiped Claim Is Unreferenced Assertion

The primary scholarly source for the geometric reading of tebah is Cassuto's commentary (1964), where he states:

"The original signification common to them all seems to have been: an object made in the shape of a parallelepiped, and this is precisely the primary meaning of the Hebrew word tēbhā, which is used also today as a term for this geometrical form. Undoubtedly the Biblical narrative refers to such a structural shape and not to that of a ship." (Cassuto, Genesis Part 2, p. 60)

Three problems:

  1. No philological source is cited for the geometric meaning. Cassuto asserts "precisely" without etymological derivation, cognate evidence, or referenced authority.
  2. It is a commentary, not a research paper. Cassuto's Commentary on Genesis is an interpretive work, not a peer-reviewed philological study. Its assertions on word etymology carry no more weight than the evidence supplied — which here is none.
  3. The appeal to modern Hebrew usage proves nothing. That tebah is used in modern Hebrew as a geometric term for parallelepiped does not establish that this was its ancient meaning. Modern usage frequently reflects the influence of the very tradition under examination — a circular argument.

"Precisely" is the word of an author asserting confidence in the absence of evidence.

7. Cohen (1972) — The Philological Rebuttal

Chayim Cohen, "Hebrew tbh: Proposed Etymologies," Journal of the Ancient Near Eastern Society 4 (1972): 37–51.

Cohen surveys all proposed etymologies — Egyptian, Akkadian, Eblaite — and concludes that none of the donor languages supply a geometric meaning. The functional reading (life-preserving vessel) is the only conclusion the linguistic evidence supports. Cohen was a specialist Hebrew linguist publishing in the field's dedicated peer-reviewed journal. He postdates Cassuto by eight years and directly addresses the etymology Cassuto assumed without argument.

Cohen subsequently confirmed to T. Lovett (~2004) that he still stood by his conclusions. He died in 2017 (Ben-Gurion University).

Spoelstra (2013) cites Cohen (p. 5, fn. 10) but does not engage his conclusions against Cassuto's parallelepiped claim. Citing both without resolving the conflict is a significant weakness in the dissertation.

8. Spoelstra (2020) — Useful Ally, Incomplete Argument

Joshua Joel Spoelstra, Life Preservation in Genesis and Exodus: An Exegetical Study of the Tēbâ of Noah and Moses. Contributions to Biblical Exegesis and Theology 98. Leuven: Peeters, 2020. (Published from his 2013 Stellenbosch PhD dissertation.)

Spoelstra's central conclusion — tebah is a terminus technicus for a life-preserving receptacle — directly supports the functional reading. His donor language analysis (Egyptian ḏbȝ.t: coffer/coffin/Götterschrein) confirms no geometric content in the word's origin.

However Spoelstra then accepts the parallelepiped/box shape from the dimensions, citing Cassuto, without rebutting Cohen's demolition of that claim. This is inconsistent: if the word is functionally defined with no geometric content, the dimensions (300×50×30) specify size, not shape. Spoelstra conflates the two.

The Götterschrein reading (divine shrine/sacred structure) is theologically rich and worth engaging — but it reinforces the functional argument, not the geometric one.

9. The Positive Case — Shape from Naval Architecture

Genesis 6:15 provides the only dimensional information for Noah's vessel: 300×50×30 cubits. At the Royal Egyptian Cubit of 524mm this gives 157.2m × 26.2m × 15.72m.

These are dimensions — an envelope specifying maximum extent in three axes. They do not specify cross-sectional shape. A naval architect reading 300×50×30 does not see a rectangular box; they see a design space within which hull form must be determined by engineering requirements: stability, seakeeping, structural efficiency, and wave behaviour.

Hong et al. (1994) confirmed the proportions are near-optimal across multiple naval architecture criteria. The proportions are those of a seagoing hull — not a barge. The shape is determined by the engineering, not by the word tebah and not by the dimensions alone.

10. The English Word "Ark" Is the Culprit

The box-shape assumption did not enter the tradition through the Hebrew tebah or even through the Greek kibotos. It entered through the English word "Ark," derived from Latin arca — chest, box, coffer. Jerome's Vulgate used arca for both Noah's vessel and Moses' basket, importing the chest connotation into the Latin tradition. English translations following the Vulgate inherited the geometric assumption.

Noah didn't build an Ark. He built what Genesis specifies: a vessel 300×50×30 cubits. The shape is an engineering question. The word tebah is silent on the matter.
