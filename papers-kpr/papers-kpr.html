Noah's Kopher — Was It Pitch?

Or Is the LXX Covering Something?

Last revised: July 2026 | Status: In development

Argument Summary

  1. Two Hebrew words for pitch — kopher (Genesis 6:14, Ark) and hemar (Genesis 11:3, Tower of Babel; Exodus 2:3, Moses basket). Different words, different materials, different geological contexts.
  2. Kopher — covering, not pitch — root kaphar = to cover/atone. Functional descriptor, not a material name. Same root as atonement.
  3. Hemar — bitumen/petroleum tar — post-flood petroleum product. Used at Babel and in Egypt. Geological context: post-flood sedimentary petroleum deposits.
  4. "Inside and out" — what does it mean? — not whole-interior coating. Structural timber specification: every plank face as laid (inside of hull construction) + outer hull face. Fitout and furnishings excluded.
  5. Construction preservation — 80-120 year build. Progressive pitch treatment as each section erected. Short exposure window per section — months not years. Standard construction maintenance.
  6. Fire assessment — pine pitch on timber passed local authority fire inspection at Creation Museum, 2007. Not the fire hazard assumed.
  7. Biological properties — pine resin as insect repellant, antimicrobial, timber preservative. Pleasant smell. Interior structural timber treatment justified on biological grounds during long build.
  8. What gets coated, what doesn't — structural timber yes; interior fitout, cages, furnishings no. Pitched as erected, not as fitted out.
  9. Geological implication — kopher = pre-flood pine resin. hemar = post-flood petroleum. The switch from one to the other reflects genuine geological discontinuity at the flood boundary.

1. Two Hebrew Words for Pitch

Genesis uses two distinct Hebrew words for pitch-like substances:

WordTransliterationReferenceContext
כֹּפֶרkopherGenesis 6:14Noah's Ark — "pitch it inside and out with kopher"
חֵמָרhemarGenesis 11:3Tower of Babel — "bitumen they used for mortar"
חֵמָרhemarExodus 2:3Moses basket — "daubed it with hemar and pitch"

These are not synonyms. They are different words for different materials used in different geological contexts. The distinction is not stylistic — it is material and geological.

2. Kopher — Covering, Not Pitch

Kopher (כֹּפֶר) derives from the root kaphar (כָּפַר) — to cover, to atone, to make propitiation. The same root gives:

WordMeaning
kapharto cover, to atone
kippurimatonements (Yom Kippur)
kapporethmercy seat — the covering of the Ark of the Covenant
kophercovering — ransom, pitch, henna

Kopher is a functional descriptor — it describes what the substance does (covers/seals) not what it is chemically. In Genesis 6:14 it refers to a covering substance appropriate for sealing a timber vessel — most plausibly pine resin pitch, the standard timber sealant of the ancient world.

The word occurs 17 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its material meaning — pitch — occurs exactly once: Genesis 6:14. The remaining 16 occurrences are:

MeaningOccurrencesDomain
Ransom — price paid to cover/redeem a life8×Legal/theological
Satisfaction — settlement of a claim2×Legal
Bribe — illicit covering of a matter2×Legal
Henna plant — used for dyeing/covering skin2×Botanical
Village — covered/sheltered settlement1×Geographical
Sum of money1×Legal
Pitch — waterproofing material1× — Genesis 6:14 onlyMaterial (pre-flood)

The dominant usage is ransom/atonement — 13 of 17 occurrences. The pitch translation of Genesis 6:14 rests entirely on context: you are building a boat, you need waterproofing, therefore the covering substance must be pitch. The word itself says only: cover it with covering. A vanilla rendering of Genesis 6:14b is:

"...and cover it inside and out with covering."

The material identity — pine resin — is the most plausible inference from ancient shipbuilding practice and the pre-flood geological context argued in section 9. But it is an inference from context, not a derivation from the word. The word is permanently generic: kopher is what you cover things with. What specifically Noah covered the Ark with is the engineering question this paper addresses.

The theological resonance is not accidental — the word for the substance that preserved Noah from the flood is the same root as the word for atonement that preserves humanity from judgement. The covering of the Ark and the covering of sin share a root. This is deliberate Hebrew wordplay embedded in the text.

3. Hemar — Bitumen/Petroleum Tar

Hemar (חֵמָר) is the standard Hebrew word for bitumen — petroleum-derived asphalt. It appears in:

ReferenceContextGeological implication
Genesis 11:3Tower of Babel mortarPost-flood Mesopotamia — surface bitumen seeps from petroleum deposits
Genesis 14:10Bitumen pits of Siddim valleySurface petroleum deposits — post-flood sedimentary context
Exodus 2:3Moses basket — hemar and pitchEgyptian Delta — petroleum-rich post-flood sediments

Petroleum bitumen is a post-flood material. It forms from organic material buried under flood sediments, subjected to heat and pressure over time. Pre-flood, petroleum deposits did not exist in accessible surface form — the organic material was still living biomass, not yet buried and transformed.

Noah could not have used hemar — it did not exist as an accessible material before the flood. Genesis is consistent: the Ark uses kopher (pre-flood pine resin); post-flood construction uses hemar (petroleum bitumen now accessible from flood sediment deposits).

4. "Inside and Out" — What Does It Mean?

Genesis 6:14: "Make yourself an ark of gopher wood... and cover it inside and out with kopher."

The standard reading assumes this means coating the entire interior of the vessel — all surfaces, all timber. This raises practical objections:

ObjectionAssessment
Sticky interior — impractical for habitationValid if fresh; cured pine resin is hard and relatively non-tacky
Fire hazardPine pitch on timber passed local authority fire inspection, Creation Museum, 2007. Not the hazard assumed.
Toxic fumesPine resin — pleasant smell, not toxic. Non-issue.
Enormous quantity of pitch requiredValid — coating every interior surface of a 157m vessel is a massive undertaking

The engineering reading: "inside and out" refers to the hull skin construction — the structural envelope — not the general interior:

SurfaceCovered?Purpose
Outer hull faceYes — "out"Waterproofing, wave abrasion protection
Every plank face within hull constructionYes — "in"Inter-plank sealing, structural timber preservation
Inner face of hull skin (ceiling planking)Yes — innermost face of "in"Moisture barrier, timber preservation
Interior structural timber — decks, frames, bulkheads, postsYes — structural specificationTimber preservation during long build
Interior fitout — cages, troughs, partitions, furnishingsNoNot structural; fitted after hull completion

"Inside and out" = complete treatment of all structural timber. Fitout is excluded — it goes in after the structure is complete and doesn't require pitch treatment for a 371-day voyage.

5. Construction Preservation — 80-120 Year Build

The Ark was built over an estimated 80-120 years. This fundamentally changes the pitch treatment argument — it is not a single coating event but an ongoing construction maintenance programme.

Construction sequence:

  1. Section of hull framing erected
  2. Exposed for weeks to months while adjacent sections completed
  3. Planking applied — each plank pitched on both faces as laid
  4. Next planking layer applied over pitched surface
  5. Repeat for all 5 layers — pitch between every layer
  6. Section covered by subsequent construction — exposure period ends

Each piece of structural timber is exposed for months, not decades. Progressive pitch treatment during this short window is standard construction practice — not an additional burden but part of the workflow.

By launch day the entire hull structure is a fully cured, pitch-impregnated timber matrix — not a surface coating but a complete penetration treatment built up over the construction period.

6. Fire Assessment

A fire safety assessment of pine pitch (genuine pine resin) applied as a thick coating to pine timber was conducted at the Creation Museum, 2007, in connection with the Noah's Ark exhibit construction. Local fire authority inspection passed the treatment as acceptable under applicable building codes.

The Great Republic (1853) — Donald McKay's masterpiece, largest wooden sailing ship of her era — burned at the New York dock before her maiden voyage when a neighbouring building fire caught the pitch-saturated rigging and sails. She burned to the waterline overnight; the rebuilt vessel was two-thirds the original size.

The Great Republic comparison applies to fresh pitch on canvas sails and rigging — not to cured pine resin impregnated into structural timber over decades of construction. The two situations are materially different. The 2007 Creation Museum fire assessment confirms this distinction.

7. Biological Properties of Pine Resin

Pine resin pitch has well-documented biological properties beyond waterproofing:

PropertyRelevance to Ark
Insect repellant80-120 year build — protects structural timber from wood-boring insects throughout construction
AntimicrobialInhibits fungal and bacterial colonisation of timber during build and voyage
Timber preservativePenetrates grain, seals moisture pathways, extends timber life
Pleasant aromaCedar and pine aromatics — beneficial air quality in enclosed vessel
Non-toxicPine resin fumes not hazardous — unlike petroleum-based pitch

The biological protection argument is strongest for the construction period — 80-120 years of exposure to insects and fungi is a genuine risk to structural timber. Progressive pitch treatment during construction addresses this directly.

9. Geological Implication

The switch from kopher (Genesis 6) to hemar (Genesis 11, 14; Exodus 2) reflects a genuine geological discontinuity at the flood boundary:

PeriodWordMaterialSource
Pre-floodkopherPine resin pitchLiving forests — resin tapped from trees
Post-floodhemarPetroleum bitumenFlood sediments — buried organic material transformed to petroleum, now accessible at surface seeps

Pre-flood: no accessible petroleum. Organic material is living biomass, not yet buried. The available sealant is biological — pine resin from living trees.

Post-flood: flood sediments have buried vast quantities of organic material. Over time (accelerated in the catastrophic plate tectonics model) this produces petroleum. Surface seeps become available — Mesopotamian bitumen pits (Genesis 14:10), Babel mortar (Genesis 11:3), Egyptian Delta bitumen (Exodus 2:3).

Genesis is not inconsistent in using two different words. It is precisely consistent — recording the material reality of two different geological epochs.

10. Word Extinction — Kopher as Hapax Noun

The noun kopher meaning pitch occurs exactly once in the entire Old Testament — Genesis 6:14. It is never used again for a physical waterproofing material. This is not coincidence — it is word extinction tracking material extinction.

The *kpr* word family in the Old Testament:

The material kopher — pre-flood pine resin — disappears from the language after Genesis 6:14 because the material itself disappeared. The pre-flood forests that produced it were buried under flood sediments. No trees, no resin, no word.

But the smearing action was too theologically useful to lose. The physical act of kafar with kopher — coating the Ark inside and out against the waters of divine judgment — became the template for the entire sacrificial vocabulary of Leviticus. Atonement is kafar: covering sin against divine judgment, as pitch covered the Ark against floodwater. The material vanished; the theological action it pictured became the central concept of the Hebrew sacrificial system.

Noah's pitch coat is the original atonement picture. The Ark sealed against judgment by kopher; the sinner sealed against judgment by kafar. Same root, same action, same protective purpose — the physical act in Genesis 6 prefiguring the theological reality that dominates the rest of the Old Testament.

Compare gopher wood — same fate for the species name, but without the theological residue. Gopher disappears entirely from the language. It had no action to carry forward, only a material identity. When the species went extinct, the word went extinct with it. See gopher wood paper.

11. Why Cloth Is Not an Option

A question arises whether kopher might have been applied to cloth — producing a pitched fabric membrane between planking layers, as Chinese shipbuilders used tung-oil impregnated cloth. This is ruled out on both linguistic and practical grounds.

Linguistically: The kpr root never appears in the Old Testament in connection with fabric or cloth. The Tabernacle coverings use entirely different vocabulary — yeri'ah (יְרִיעָה) for curtains, mikseh (מִכְסֶה) for the outer covering. The kpr root touches timber, skin, and theological concepts — never fabric. There is no linguistic basis for a cloth application.

Practically: The hull construction uses thousands of trunnel (wooden peg) fastenings driven through multiple planking layers — outer planking through to ceiling strakes, steel bolted from inner ceiling to third planking layer. Any cloth membrane between planking layers would be perforated at every fastening point. A membrane pierced thousands of times is not a membrane.

Structurally unnecessary: Five layers of edge-jointed planking with mortise-and-tenon joints swell under water immersion, closing gaps and self-sealing. The hull skin does not need an additional membrane — the kopher applied directly to timber surfaces inside and out is the complete waterproofing specification.

The text says inside and out — timber surfaces. That is what kafar means: to smear, to coat, to apply directly to a surface. No intermediary material is linguistically warranted or practically useful.
