Two interactive browser-based simulators are planned for this site, both derived from original VB6 programs written by T. Lovett in 2004–2005 for worldwideflood.com. The physics and algorithms are proven — the task is reimplementation in HTML5 canvas and JavaScript for modern browsers without requiring any download or installation.

Both simulators use the same parameterised hull cross-section: bilge radius, deadrise, flare/tumblehome, and beam/depth ratios. Both accept cubit selection as the primary dimension input, scaling all geometry accordingly.

Simulator 1 — GZ Curve Plotter

Predecessor: Hydrostatic Calculator Ver 5, T. Lovett, May 2005

The GZ curve plotter calculates static roll stability — the righting arm GZ as a function of heel angle for a given hull cross-section and loading condition. This is the first check performed on any new hull design. It makes no assumptions about dynamics — it answers only: if this vessel is heeled to angle θ and released in calm water, does it return upright?

Physics

The calculation follows Archimedes' principle applied to a polygonal hull cross-section:

  1. Define hull geometry — polygonal cross-section from input parameters (beam, depth, bilge radius, deadrise, flare/tumblehome, roof camber)
  2. Apply heel angle — rotate hull polygon to current roll angle θ
  3. Calculate submerged area — trapezoidal integration of the waterplane-clipped polygon. For any polygon with vertices in consecutive order:
    subarea += (Xᵢ − Xᵢ₊₁) × (Yᵢ₊₁ + Yᵢ) / 2
  4. Vertical force equilibrium — shift hull vertically until buoyancy force = gravity force. Newton-Raphson iteration converges in 3–4 steps to accuracy < 10⁻⁹
  5. Buoyancy centroid B — centroid of submerged polygon:
    k = (Xᵢ × Yᵢ₊₁) − (Xᵢ₊₁ × Yᵢ)
    Xb = Σ((Xᵢ + Xᵢ₊₁) × k) / (6 × subarea)
  6. GZ — horizontal distance between B and G (gravity centre)
  7. Repeat for θ = 0° to 90° at selected interval
  8. Integrate GZ curve — trapezoidal sum gives area under GZ curve (energy to capsize, m·rad)

This method handles any polygonal cross-section at any heel angle without special geometric cases. A bilge radius is approximated by a polygon with sufficient vertices to give smooth curves.

Validation

The predecessor VB6 program reproduced Hong et al. (1994) Table 3 stability data (column AR) to better than 0.1% at 1000 intervals for all 13 hull forms. The HTML reimplementation will use the same algorithm and validate against the same dataset before release.

Simulator 2 — Dynamic Roll Simulator

Predecessor: Noah's Ark Beam Sea Simulator (Pre-release), T. Lovett, Jan 2004

The dynamic roll simulator extends the static GZ calculation into time-domain simulation. Given a wave environment and initial conditions, it integrates the equations of motion forward in time — computing roll, heave and sway at each timestep and animating the hull cross-section in real time. This is the tool for evaluating seakeeping performance, resonance risk, and the effectiveness of roll damping systems including the triple keel.

Physics

At each timestep the following sequence is executed:

  1. Wave surface — compute instantaneous water surface elevation at each point of the hull from the selected wave model (sinusoidal, Stokes, wave group, or opposing waves)
  2. Wave particle velocities — orbital motion of water particles (circular at surface, decaying exponentially with depth). These velocities define the relative velocity of hull surfaces through the water — essential for physically correct damping
  3. Submerged geometry — intersect current hull position (roll + heave + sway) with instantaneous wave surface. Same polygonal algorithm as Simulator 1
  4. Buoyancy force and moment — from submerged area centroid
  5. Froude-Krylov force — pressure field of undisturbed wave acting on hull — primary wave excitation force
  6. Damping forces — quadratic drag on hull surfaces and keels: F_drag = ½ ρ C_d A v² where v is instantaneous surface velocity relative to water particle velocity. Physically derived — not a fudged linear damping coefficient
  7. Wind force — adjustable constant heel moment
  8. Equations of motion — resolve net forces and moments into angular acceleration (roll), vertical acceleration (heave) and lateral acceleration (sway) using hull mass and radii of gyration
  9. Numerical integration — Euler or RK4 timestep. Add accelerations to velocities, velocities to positions
  10. Render — draw hull cross-section at new position, waterline, wave surface, force vectors

Damping Model

The predecessor program used adjustable linear damping coefficients (Roll Damp, Heave Damp, Sway Damp) that were acknowledged as uncalibrated. The new simulator replaces these with physically derived quadratic damping:

ComponentMechanismParameters
Hull skin frictionViscous drag on wetted surface — velocity-dependentC_d for smooth timber surface
Bilge dampingSeparation drag at bilge — dominant for bare hullC_d for rounded bilge
Keel dampingQuadratic drag on keel plan areaC_d ≈ 1.0–1.5 (flat plate normal to flow); keel area adjustable
Triple keel configurationThree keels at keel, port bilge, starboard bilgeEmpirical ζ ≈ 0.10–0.18 from literature

The damping coefficient ζ emerging from the simulation can be compared against empirical values from keel damping literature. This is the calibration check the predecessor program lacked.

Extension to Strip Method

The 2D midship strip simulation is the foundation for a 3D strip method analysis. Once the single-strip physics is validated, the hull can be divided into N transverse strips along its length. Each strip runs the same physics independently, summing contributions to total force and moment. This captures:

  • Phase differences in wave excitation along the hull length
  • Variation in hull geometry from midship to bow and stern
  • Longitudinal bending moment distribution — feeding directly into the structural analysis
  • Pitch and heave coupling with roll

The strip method is the standard naval architecture tool for seakeeping prediction. Implementing it in the browser for a parameterised hull form would be a genuine research contribution — no equivalent tool currently exists for public use.
