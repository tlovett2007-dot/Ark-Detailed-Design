The Ark of Noah Revisited

Correcting the Naval Architecture of Morris (1971)

T. Lovett | Planned — not yet in development | 2026

Henry Morris's 1971 CRSQ paper "The Ark of Noah" contains several naval architecture claims that require correction. Most significantly: Morris asserted that the Ark would naturally align bow-first into oncoming waves. This is correct behaviour for a ship-shaped hull with appropriate stern keels and freeboard asymmetry. It is not correct for a rectangular box hull, which has no directional stability and defaults to beam-on orientation. Morris described the right seakeeping behaviour and attributed it to the wrong vessel. This paper corrects the naval architecture record while acknowledging what Morris got right.

Background

Henry Morris, "The Ark of Noah," Creation Research Society Quarterly 8(2):142–144, 1971. The first creationist engineering paper specifically addressing Ark stability and seakeeping. Morris was a hydraulic engineer — not a naval architect — working before the Hong et al. (1994) proportions study and before modern computational seakeeping analysis was available to creationist researchers.

The 1971 paper is distinct from Whitcomb and Morris (1961), The Genesis Flood, which assumed a box hull for volume calculation convenience without making specific naval architecture claims. The 1971 paper goes further — making specific seakeeping assertions that are demonstrably wrong.

Claims to be Addressed

1. Directional Stability — The Central Error

⚠ Open Item 1 — Locate exact Morris (1971) quote asserting the Ark would naturally align with waves / point bow-first into weather. Confirm the exact claim before rebuttal.

Morris asserted that the Ark would naturally orient itself with respect to oncoming waves — effectively a passive weathervaning claim. This is correct behaviour for a vessel with:

  • Stern keels providing drag at the weather end
  • High bow freeboard acting as a wind vane tail
  • Asymmetric hull form bow-to-stern

A rectangular box hull has none of these properties. All four sides present equal projected area to wind and wave forces. There is no restoring moment that returns the vessel to any preferred orientation. The default condition for a box hull in any sea state is beam-on — the worst possible orientation, maximising rolling and risk of swamping.

Morris described the correct seakeeping behaviour — passive alignment with wave direction — and attributed it to a hull form that cannot produce it. The AiG Ark Encounter, with its stern keels and bow/stern asymmetry, is the hull form that actually produces the behaviour Morris described.

2. Unsinkability

⚠ Open Item 2 — Locate exact Morris claim regarding unsinkability or exceptional stability. Confirm the exact wording.

No vessel is unsinkable. The stability question at Ark scale requires proper hydrostatic analysis — GZ curves at large angles of heel, free surface effects of water in compartments, wave-induced dynamic loading. Morris's stability calculation was simplified. Hong et al. (1994) performed the first rigorous stability analysis and found the proportions near-optimal — but this is a proportions result, not an unsinkability claim.

3. Proportions — Right Conclusion, Wrong Reasoning

Morris was correct that the Genesis proportions are well-suited for seakeeping. He was wrong about why. The proportions are near-optimal because:

  • The 6:1 length-to-beam ratio provides directional stability in following seas — but only for a ship-shaped hull with weathervaning features
  • The depth-to-beam ratio provides adequate metacentric height
  • The overall proportions minimise longitudinal bending moment relative to cross-sectional area

Hong et al. (1994) confirmed the proportions rigorously. Morris asserted them. The conclusion is the same; the methodology is not.

What Morris Got Right

  1. The seakeeping question is legitimate — Morris was right to ask whether the Ark could survive open-ocean conditions. The question is valid; the analysis was insufficient.
  2. The proportions are good — confirmed by Hong et al. (1994). Morris's intuition was correct.
  3. Passive operation is required — Morris recognised that the Ark had to survive without active crew intervention. Correct — and this is precisely why hull form matters so much.
  4. The vessel needed directional stability — Morris recognised this need. He was wrong about which hull form provides it.

Conclusion — Stub

"Morris described a vessel that would naturally weather the flood. He then specified a hull form that would not. The AiG Ark Encounter is the engineering correction his paper required."
