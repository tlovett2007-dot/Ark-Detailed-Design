Argument Summary

  1. Hong et al. (1994) — the foundational study — nine KRISO naval architects confirmed Ark feasibility. Peer-reviewed, rigorous, influential. The starting point for all subsequent work.
  2. Limitation 1 — Timber modelled as steel — membrane elements (planking) and truss elements (frames). Steel FEA methodology applied to timber without adjustment. Misses bending stiffness and composite action entirely.
  3. Limitation 2 — Confused sea assumption — all wave directions equally probable. Ignores weathervaning. Organised following sea is significantly less severe.
  4. Limitation 3 — Box hull throughout — Cb ≈ 1.0, all 13 comparative hulls rectangular cross-section. Ship-shaped hull never tested.
  5. Limitation 4 — Roll explicitly unmodelled — Hong's team acknowledged roll as nonlinear/viscous and unreliable. The most critical motion for animal welfare.
  6. Limitation 5 — Transcription error in English Table 3 — AR and Safety Index columns contain errors. Corrected values derived from Hong's own data.
  7. Each limitation makes Hong conservative — every gap in the analysis understates Ark performance. The study makes the weakest possible case and still confirms feasibility.
  8. Conclusion — Hong et al. is a landmark study that understates its own positive result. The actual vessel outperforms the model on every metric.

1. Hong et al. (1994) — The Foundational Study

Hong SW, Na SS, Hyun BS, Hong SY, Gong DS, Kang KJ, Suh SH, Lee KH, Je YG (1994). Safety investigation of Noah's Ark in a seaway. CEN Technical Journal 8(1):26–36.

Nine researchers from the Korea Research Institute of Ships and Ocean Engineering (KRISO) — a world-class ship research facility — applied professional naval architecture methodology to the biblical dimensions of Noah's Ark. Three safety parameters were evaluated:

ParameterMethodResult
Structural safetyANSYS FEA — wave load analysisArk had high structural safety — 30cm timber adequate for 30m waves
Overturning stabilityGZ curve analysis — ABS criteriaArk 13× more stable than ABS passenger ship requirement
Seakeeping qualityStrip method — 6 DOF motionsVoyage limit >43m waves (vertical acceleration) and >47.5m (roll flooding)

The study compared 13 hull forms at equal displacement by varying L, B, and D proportions. The biblical Ark ranked near the top across all weighting schemes. This is the result of antagonistic witnesses — KRISO is not a creation science organisation; their confirmation of Ark feasibility carries significant evidential weight.

The present paper does not challenge Hong's conclusions. It demonstrates that each identified limitation makes the study more conservative than the actual vessel warrants — the positive result is stronger than Hong's analysis shows.

2. Limitation 1 — Timber Modelled as Steel

Hong's structural analysis used ANSYS with two element types:

Element typeUsed forBehaviour modelledBehaviour excluded
Truss elementsFrame structureAxial load only — tension and compression along member axisBending stiffness — frames cannot resist moment
Membrane/diaphragm elementsPlanking and decksIn-plane tension and compression onlyOut-of-plane bending stiffness — planking cannot resist transverse load by bending

These are standard steel structural element types. In steel construction they are appropriate — steel plates are thin relative to span, so membrane behaviour dominates and bending stiffness is negligible. Applied to timber, they are a category error:

Timber planking realityMembrane element assumption
127mm thick × span — significant bending stiffnessZero bending stiffness — pure membrane
Cross-laminated layers — orthotropic compositeIsotropic membrane — no grain direction
Edge-jointed (mortise and tenon) — panels act compositelyNo joint contribution — each element independent
Stressed-skin action — planking and frames unifiedNo composite action — frame carries frame load, membrane carries membrane load
Bending across grain resisted by each plankNo cross-grain bending resistance

The result: Hong's ANSYS model is a steel space frame with a thin membrane skin — not a wooden ship. The model significantly understates the stiffness and load-carrying capacity of the actual construction.

A correctly modelled timber hull — with shell elements having appropriate bending stiffness, orthotropic material properties, and composite frame-planking action — would show substantially lower stresses and higher safety margins than Hong's analysis produced. Hong's structural safety result is therefore conservative by a significant but unquantified margin.

The Stressed-Skin Principle

The Ark's construction uses plank-first shell construction — the Mediterranean tradition descending from mortise-and-tenon joinery. In this system the planking is not a covering applied to a frame; it is the primary structural member, with frames acting as stiffeners. The load path is:

Wave pressure → planking skin (bending + membrane) → frame stiffeners → bulkheads → overall hull girder

Hong's membrane elements eliminate the first and most important load path entirely. The analysis jumps directly to the frame/girder level — a level that in stressed-skin construction carries a fraction of the total load.

3. Limitation 2 — Confused Sea Assumption

Hong states: "The Ark was supposed to have drifted at a very low speed" and "Since the waves came from all directions with the same probability, we defined another safety index..." This confused sea assumption — all wave directions equally probable — is the most conservative possible wave environment for a drifting vessel.

In reality, a vessel with passive directional stability (weathervaning) aligns itself with the dominant wave direction. The Ark's hull form — deep stern keels, low stern freeboard, high bow freeboard, stern trim — provides exactly this passive heading alignment. The result is an organised following sea, not a confused sea.

ParameterConfused sea (Hong)Organised following sea (actual)
Worst wave headingBeam sea — occurs with equal probability to all other headingsFollowing sea — vessel aligned stern-to-waves
Roll excitationMaximum — beam seas fully excite rollMinimum — following seas produce pitch, not roll
SlammingOccurs from all directionsReduced — bow protected by weathervaning
Deck wettingAll headings contributeReduced — stern takes wave energy, bow protected
Structural loadsMaximum — worst heading always presentReduced — organised sea has defined wave direction

The Hull 9 paradox illustrates this directly. Hull 9 (shorter, wider: L/1.5 × 1.5B × D) outperforms the biblical Ark in Hong's confused sea — its greater beam gives better stability and the shorter length reduces structural bending moment. But in an organised following sea, the Ark's greater length is an asset: longer vessels average out wave orbital velocities over more wave lengths, reducing effective wave slope, while Hull 9's shorter length means it rides individual wave slopes more acutely.

Hong's confused sea assumption explains why the Ark appears sub-optimal in his analysis. The biblical proportions are optimised for an organised wind-driven sea — the actual Genesis flood condition.

4. Limitation 3 — Box Hull Throughout

Hong's paper states explicitly: "Since all hull forms in this study had a rectangular cross section, the GZ curve could be determined analytically..." All 13 comparative hulls used rectangular cross-sections. The study varied L, B, and D proportions only — cross-sectional shape was never a variable.

The KACR (Korea Association of Creation Research) hull form provided to Hong had a slightly modified bilge radius and dead rise — but the ANSYS structural model used rectangular cross-sections throughout (Cb ≈ 1.0 for the displacement calculation: Δ = 1.025 × L × B × d with no block coefficient deduction).

A ship-shaped cross-section — curved bilge, dead rise, bow flare, stern taper — affects all three safety parameters:

ParameterBox hullShip-shaped hull
Structural — bilge stressSharp bilge corner — stress concentration in timber, potentially unbuildableCurved bilge — membrane/hoop stress, load distributed efficiently
Stability — GZ curveSharp loss of waterplane at large heel — GZ falls steeplyProgressive loss — more forgiving GZ curve at large angles
Seakeeping — rollHigh added mass coefficient — stiff roll, short periodLower added mass — softer roll, longer period, lower accelerations
Seakeeping — pitchFull bow waterplane — high pitch exciting momentFine bow entry — reduced pitch exciting moment

The structural implication is particularly significant. A sharp bilge corner in timber construction creates a stress concentration that may be physically unrealisable — the grain direction changes abruptly, creating a weakness exactly where hydrostatic pressure is highest. Hong's rectangular model assumes this corner exists and carries load — but the actual construction would use a curved bilge precisely to avoid it.

5. Limitation 4 — Roll Explicitly Unmodelled

Hong's team explicitly acknowledged the roll limitation: "Good agreement was obtained for all motions except roll motion, which usually showed strong nonlinear behaviour due to viscous damping. This discrepancy in roll motion would not have created serious problems, since in this research we put stress on the relative safety concept."

Roll is the most critical motion for animal welfare aboard the Ark. Lateral acceleration determines whether animals lose footing, injure themselves against stall walls, or pile up against bulkheads. Hong's roll analysis — based on linear strip theory — systematically underestimates roll damping and overestimates roll amplitude because:

FactorLinear model (Hong)Actual behaviour
Viscous dampingNot modelled — acknowledged gapDominant at moderate-large roll angles — provides significant damping
Bilge keel dampingNot includedDeep stern skegs approximately double roll damping (ζ ≈ 0.10–0.18 vs ζ ≈ 0.05–0.08 bare hull)
Moon pool dampingNot modelledTwin stern moon pools contribute roll damping via U-tube anti-roll tank principle
Large amplitude behaviourLinear — constant damping coefficientNonlinear — damping increases with amplitude, self-limiting

Hong's roll safety index for the Ark (0.68 out of 1.0, where 0 = safest) reflects this limitation — roll was the weakest parameter in their analysis precisely because it was the least accurately modelled. The actual roll behaviour, with viscous damping, stern keel contribution, and moon pool action, would show significantly lower amplitudes and better animal welfare outcomes.

7. Each Limitation Makes Hong Conservative

The critical observation: every identified limitation causes Hong's analysis to understate Ark performance. There are no identified limitations that make the analysis optimistic.

LimitationDirection of errorActual performance vs modelled
Timber as membrane/trussUnderstates structural stiffnessActual structure stiffer and stronger
Confused seaOverstates wave severityOrganised following sea is less severe
Box hull (Cb=1.0)Worst-case cross-sectionShip-shaped hull performs better on all metrics
Roll unmodelledOverstates roll amplitudeActual roll damping higher — lower amplitudes
Transcription errorNeutral — does not affect Ark valuesNo effect on conclusions

A study that confirmed feasibility using the most conservative possible assumptions at every decision point is a stronger confirmation than one using best-estimate assumptions. Hong et al. made the hardest possible case for Ark feasibility — and still confirmed it.

8. Conclusion

Hong et al. (1994) is a landmark study — the first peer-reviewed naval architecture analysis of Noah's Ark, conducted by world-class researchers at a credible institution. Its positive findings deserve full weight precisely because they were obtained conservatively.

This paper identifies five limitations in Hong's analysis, demonstrates that each makes the result more conservative than the actual vessel warrants, and concludes that the Ark's seakeeping and structural performance exceeds what Hong's study demonstrated.

The practical implication: critics who cite Hong's analysis as the ceiling of Ark performance are misreading the study. Hong established a floor. The actual vessel sits above it on every metric.
